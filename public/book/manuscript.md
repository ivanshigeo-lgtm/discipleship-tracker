Chapter One
The Speed of Trust

There was a red glow across the clouds that evening.

You live on Maui long enough and you learn the moods of the sky, and this one was wrong. The smell came with it, smoke drifting across the West Maui mountains and settling over the whole island like a lid on a pot. People were saying things. That there were wildfires in Lahaina. That major structures were burning. Nobody could tell you how much or how bad, only that it was happening, and that the wind would not quit.

It was not only the west side. Up in Kula the fire was doing something it had never done in the history of that place, tearing through the upcountry like it had a mind of its own. Kihei was evacuated. The fire came down off the mountain and kept coming until it stopped about twenty yards from the edge of the Kihei district, and a whole town stood still and watched it decide. You could feel the island holding its breath. And then the night closed over everything, and we went to sleep not knowing, because there was nothing else to do but wait for the morning.

The morning came, and with it a video.

A friend of mine flies helicopters. He sent me footage he had taken from the air. I watched it, and then I watched it again, because the first time my mind would not accept what my eyes were telling me. Lahaina was gone. Not damaged. Gone. Smoke rising off the ashes of a town that had stood for more than a hundred years, the historic heart of the west side, the place that gave that whole coast its allure and drew something like a quarter of the tourists who come to Maui. Block after block of it, black. I could not believe what I was seeing.

I want to tell you what Lahaina was before I tell you what we did, because you cannot understand the second thing without the first.

Maui is a small place. I did not live in Lahaina, but distance does not mean much on an island this size. Everyone's town is your town. I spent weekends over there my whole life. Dates with my wife, walking Front Street, the two of us with the water on one side. My kids in the sand at the beach. My high school years out in the surf with my friends, paddling out at spots I could still find with my eyes closed. Lahaina was stitched into my memory the way a place is when you have loved it in every season of your life. It was a place I loved. When I watched that video, I was not watching a news story about strangers. I was watching part of my own life burn.

And I knew, the way you sometimes know a thing all at once and without argument, that we had to do something.

So we opened the church. Grace Bible Church became a place where people could bring what they had and take what they needed, no cost, no questions. Donations came in, and we started helping in the only way that made sense in those first hours, which was to feed the people who were running toward the fire when everyone else was running away. We got meals to our first responders. And as we put our hands to work, we started to hear. That is how it goes. You do not learn what people need by planning it in a meeting. You learn it by showing up, by being close enough that they can tell you. Water. Medical supplies. Food. Gas. So we told our community what was needed, and our community answered, and we began collecting truckloads of supplies and moving them where they had to go.

Then came the stories.

I heard about one family. The father worked on a boat, and the boat was sunk and burned. The mother worked at a school, and the school burned to the ground. Their home was destroyed. And the children's school no longer existed. Any one of those things would be enough to break a family for a year. This family lived through four of them in a single evening. And they were not alone. All over the west side, people had lost everything they owned, and many of them had lost people too, family members who did not make it out. The scale of it does not fit inside your chest. You cannot hold it. You can only start somewhere.

Where we started was our own people.

We decided to get money into hands as fast as we could, and we decided we were not going to wait behind a wall of paperwork to do it. Most organizations, and for good reason, build a process to protect themselves from fraud, and that process takes time, and time was the one thing the people of Lahaina did not have. We had something else. We had the speed of relationships. We had the speed of trust. Our congregation was full of people who personally knew families who had lost everything, so we put the checks in the hands of the people who knew the people. Five hundred dollars at a time, through members who could look someone in the eye and say, this is for you. In short order we had written out more than a hundred thousand dollars that way.

I will be honest, we did not know how it would land. Five hundred dollars against a burned-down life is nothing. It felt almost like an insult to the size of the loss. I worried about how people would take it. But our members came back with the same report, over and over. People received those checks with gratitude. With tears. Not because the amount solved anything, but because someone had seen them, and reached them, and reached them fast.

Around that time I started hearing about the influencers. People online raising money for Maui, hundreds of thousands of dollars flowing to accounts with big followings, and folks kept telling me we needed to do the same, we needed to get out there and post and campaign and pull in those kinds of numbers. I prayed about it. And what I heard back was clear enough that I have never forgotten it. God told me those influencers were raising money for us. That if we did not get distracted, if we just kept our heads down and kept helping the community in front of us, the money would find its way to us in the end.

So that is what we did. We did not have enough. We kept giving anyway. And as we gave, more came in. It kept pouring in. We ended up putting five hundred dollars each into the hands of more than a thousand families, and we cleared half a million dollars in the first couple of months alone. Then the need changed, the way a wound changes as it moves through its stages. First it was water and food and gas. Then it was housing. And the funds shifted with it, and we were able to help churches set up feeding kitchens, help rebuild what had burned, help people get back on their feet. But that is further down the road than this chapter goes. For now I only want you to see the shape of those first weeks: a town in ashes, and a people moving at the speed of trust, and money arriving from strangers exactly the way I had been told it would.

Which brings me to my journal.

I keep my devotions in a simple form. Scripture, observation, application, prayer. I have done it for years. And in an ordinary season I write it most mornings and the dates line up neatly, one day after another. But that fall was not an ordinary season, and I fell behind. For months I fell behind. There was no room in those days for a quiet notebook. There were people sleeping in the church and people I was sitting with in their sorrow and truckloads to move and checks to write, and the mornings got swallowed one after another.

I have a rule I keep even when everything else is coming apart. I do not skip. I catch up. When I finally get an hour, I sit down and I write the days I missed, and I date each entry to the day it belongs to, so the sequence stays true even when the calendar and the ink no longer agree. The dates lag, but the order never breaks.

So there are pages in my journal marked early July of 2023 that were not written in July at all. They were written in September, from inside the aftermath, with Lahaina still smoking in my memory. I was walking through Jeremiah in those weeks, and I have thought about that many times since, because I did not choose the book to fit the moment. The reading plan chose it, months before there was anything to grieve. And there was the prophet, writing to a scattered people from inside a catastrophe, telling them to build houses and plant gardens in the land of their exile, because the rebuild was going to take time.

*From my journal, filed under July 8:*

"All pain and suffering is temporary. God, thank you for giving to me a perspective that is longer than the next few years. Help me to see the good that you have prepared for the many inhabitants of Lahaina and for Maui. Help me to walk with faith and not with fear knowing you are with me."

And then the next page, the one I have come to think of as the seed of everything that followed. Jeremiah is in prison, and God tells him to call out and be shown great and mighty things he does not yet know. I sat with that verse in September, thinking about my own loss and the loss of a whole town, and I wrote a prayer without any idea that I was naming a book.

*From my journal, filed under July 9:*

"In my loss and the loss of Lahaina, God wants me to call on him to show the great and mighty things. God help me to see the beauty past the ashes, that we might be trees of righteousness that He should be glorified."

Beauty past the ashes. I did not invent the phrase and I did not know at the time that I would build a book around it. It came out of Isaiah, and it came out of the ground of Lahaina, and it came out of the one thing in that burned town that would not let go of hope.

There was a banyan tree.

It stood down by the harbor and had stood there for more than a hundred years, one of the great landmarks of the place, the tree everyone photographed, the tree under which a whole town's worth of ordinary afternoons had happened. When the fire came through, it burned like everything else. It went black. And in the weeks after, people would look at it and wonder out loud whether it was dead, whether anything that scorched could ever come back, whether there was any hope left in it at all.

Then, months later, the first green shoots appeared.

The roots had gone down deep enough, over all those years, to find water the tree could not see. Everything above ground was ash and everything around it was dry, and still, down in the dark, it had been holding onto a source. And when the green came, the whole town understood what it meant without anyone having to explain it. If that tree could rise out of the ashes, maybe Lahaina could too.

That is where this book is going. I will get to the banyan tree properly later, and I will get to the widows in my church who I once thought of as victims and learned to see as heroes, and to the funerals, and to the day I found something in me had broken and needed mending. Those are stories for the chapters ahead.

But I want to end this first chapter where the prophet ended and where Isaiah pointed, because it is the truth underneath all the rest. Isaiah wrote that God would give beauty for ashes, the oil of joy for mourning, the garment of praise for the spirit of heaviness, that we would be called trees of righteousness, the planting of the Lord, that He might be glorified. The whole thing turns on one small word. For. He gives us something for something. It is an exchange.

Which means the ashes are not worthless. They have value to Him. He is asking for them.

I did not understand that yet, standing there in September with a helicopter video still burning behind my eyes and a town still smoking and a notebook full of days I was only now catching up on. But I had already written the prayer. Help me to see the beauty past the ashes. And He was already answering it, one green shoot at a time.

Chapter 2
Everyone's Town

There is a thing people who don't live on islands have a hard time understanding.

On the mainland a town can burn and the next town over will read about it in the news. Here it does not work that way. Maui is one island, and one island is one neighborhood, however many miles of road lie between its ends. The man who fixes your car went to school with the woman who ran the plate lunch place in the town that burned. Your kids play ball against their kids. The person who lost everything is not a stranger you watched on a screen. He is somebody's cousin, and on Maui everybody is somebody's cousin eventually. There is no such thing here as someone else's disaster.

That is what I mean when I say Lahaina was everyone's town. I told you already what it was to me, the weekends and the surf and the memories stitched in over a lifetime, and I don't need to say all that again. But Lahaina was not only mine. It belonged to the whole island the way a heart belongs to a whole body. It was the old capital, the historic front of the west side, the harbor and the streets that every family on Maui had walked through at one time or another. Weddings happened there. Anniversaries. The ordinary Saturdays that a life is actually made of. When people talk about Lahaina they are not really talking about buildings. They are talking about the place where a good part of the island kept its memories. So when it went, it did not go for a few thousand residents. It went for all of us. Every person on Maui lost something in that fire, even the ones whose houses never caught.

I have said the loss does not fit inside your chest, and it doesn't. You could not take it in as a total. You could only take it in one family at a time. A father whose boat was sunk and burned, a mother whose school was gone, a home destroyed, children with no school left standing — four whole tragedies in a single evening, in a single household, and that was one family among thousands. Multiply that grief across a town and your mind refuses the arithmetic. It will not hold. So you stop trying to hold the whole thing, and you do the only thing a person can do, which is to reach the family in front of you.

For us that meant the church.

I told you we opened Grace Bible Church for donations, but I want you to see what that actually became, because it was more than a drop-off point. It became a store where nothing had a price. People brought what they had, and people took what they needed, and no money changed hands and no one asked a single question at the door. You walked in, and if you needed diapers you took diapers, and if you needed clothes you took clothes, and if you needed food you took food, and you walked out again carried by the generosity of strangers who would never know your name. That is a strange and holy thing to watch, a room full of goods flowing in one door and out the other with no ledger in between. It runs against everything the world trains us to do with our possessions. And for a while, in that building, it was simply how things worked.

But a free store closes at night, and the need did not.

So we let people sleep in the church. Whole families came in off a burned coast with nowhere in the world to lay their heads, and we gave them the sanctuary floor and were glad to. And here I have to tell you something that still sits oddly with me when I say it out loud. We slept there too. My own family slept in that building, night after night, for security. The church was full of donated goods and full of people who had lost everything, and someone needed to be there through the dark hours, so we were there. There is a picture in my mind I don't think will ever leave, of the room where I preach on Sundays turned into a shelter, bodies on the floor in the quiet, and my own kids among them. I had spent years standing at the front of that room talking about the love of God. For those weeks the room itself was the sermon, and I was only one of the people asleep in it.

It was not only the church. The homes of our people opened too. Families in the congregation who had their houses still standing threw their doors wide and took in people they had never met, put them up in spare rooms and on couches and in whatever space they had, for many nights in those early days when there was simply nowhere else for anyone to go. Nobody organized it from a stage. It just happened, the way water finds the low ground. Somebody had a need and somebody else had a room, and the two found each other, and that was that. I watched my own congregation become housing before there was any housing to be had. Some of them are people I will tell you about later, and when you meet them you will understand that the ones who opened their homes widest were often the ones who had themselves been through the deepest loss. But that is a chapter for further on.

For three, four months, that was our life. Sitting with people who had lost everything. Praying with them. Being close enough to hear what was needed and then trying to meet it. There was no plan for a season like that, no manual, and honestly no rest. You did not go home at the end of the day and set it down, because for a long stretch there was no clear line between the day and the night, or between the church and the home, or between the helpers and the helped. We were all just inside the same fire together, doing the next thing that needed doing.

And some of the next things were harder to reach than others.

There is one I have to tell you about now, though I have to tell it plainly, because I was in the middle of it and I still could not give you every piece of it if you asked. Lahaina is on the far side of the West Maui mountains, connected to the rest of us by a road that hugs the coast, and in those first days that road did not always cooperate. There were times we simply could not get supplies through by land. Our caravan of trucks, loaded and ready, could not make the crossing. And so we did the thing the situation asked of us, which was to stop trying to force the road and put the supplies on the water instead. When we couldn't get to Lahaina by caravan, we got there by boat.

I wish I could set the whole scene for you the way I have set the others — what we carried, whose boat it was, where we landed on that ruined shore, whose hands received it on the other end. I was part of it and it happened and there are photographs of it, and I would rather leave the details to be told right than dress them up here from memory.

*[FOR THE INTERVIEW: The boat run — whose boat was it, what did it carry, why couldn't the caravan get through by road, where did the boat land, and who received the supplies on the Lahaina side?]*

What I can tell you is what it meant. It meant the help got there anyway. When the road said no, the water said yes, and the supplies that could not go by wheel went by hull, and reached people who could not have known or cared by what route their food and water arrived, only that it came. There is a lesson buried in that, and it is the same lesson as the influencers and the checks I told you about before. The help finds a way. You do not always get to choose the road. You only have to refuse to quit when one road closes, and trust that another one is opening while you are still standing at the barricade.

I keep coming back to those two words. Everyone's town. Because it worked in both directions, and that is the part I did not fully see until later. Lahaina belonged to all of us, which is why all of us grieved it. But the response belonged to all of us too. It was not the church that fed the west side, and it was not the influencers, and it was not any one hero you could name and thank. It was a whole island turning toward one wounded corner of itself. Boats and trucks and spare rooms and sanctuary floors and five-hundred-dollar checks passed hand to hand by people who knew the people. When a heart is hurt, the whole body sends what it has to the wound. That is not charity. That is just what a body does, if it is alive.

I did not know yet, in those crowded months, what all of it was for. I did not know that some of the people sleeping on that floor and opening those homes were being prepared for something, or that the town in ashes was the beginning of an exchange I could not yet read. I only knew that everyone's town had burned, and that everyone had come.

That turned out to be the first shoot of green, though none of us called it that at the time. We were too tired to name it. We just kept showing up, one family at a time, on whatever road was open that day.

Chapter 3
Catching Up with Jeremiah

I told you already that I keep my devotions in a simple form, and that I fell behind that fall. I want to stay there a while, because the notebook is closer to the center of this story than it looks.

The form is four letters. Scripture, observation, application, prayer. I write down the verse that met me that morning. I write what I see in it, plainly, the way you would tell a friend across a table. I write how it lands on my actual life, the one I am living that day and not some cleaner life I wish I had. And then I write a prayer, short usually, because by then there is nothing left to say but the true thing. Scripture, observation, application, prayer. I have done it for years, most mornings, in the same handwriting getting a little worse as the years go. It is not literature. Half of it would embarrass me if you read it out loud. But it is the truest record of my life that exists anywhere, because I was not writing it for anyone. I was writing it to stay found.

That is what a discipline like this actually is. People hear the word discipline and think of a man white-knuckling his way through something joyless. But a discipline is a rope you tie to yourself in the calm weather so that when the storm comes and you cannot feel your hands, the rope is already there. You do not tie it in the storm. You cannot. Your fingers do not work in the storm. You tie it years ahead, one plain morning at a time, and then one day the wind takes the roof off your life and you reach down and the rope is still knotted to your waist, because you tied it back when tying it felt like nothing at all.

When Lahaina burned, my mornings went first. There was no room for a quiet notebook in those weeks. I have told you what those weeks were, the sanctuary turned into a shelter, the checks, the truckloads, the people I sat with in their sorrow. The mornings got swallowed one after another, the way everything got swallowed. And for months the notebook sat with empty pages where the days should have been.

But I have a rule I keep even when everything else is coming apart. I do not skip. I catch up.

When I finally get an hour, I sit down and I write the days I missed, and I date each entry to the day it belongs to, not the day I am writing it. So the sequence stays true even when the calendar and the ink no longer agree. The dates lag. The order never breaks. That distinction sounds like a small bookkeeping thing, but I have come to think it is most of what faith is. You will not always keep up. There will be stretches where you cannot feel God at all, where the days blur and the prayers do not come and you could not tell someone what you believe because you are too tired to believe anything out loud. That is not the same as falling away. Falling away is breaking the sequence. Catching up is sitting down when you finally can, and refusing to let the missing days stay missing, and writing them in their proper order even though your hand is writing September while the page says July.

So that is what I did. Somewhere in September, with the town still smoking in my memory, I sat down and caught up on the summer. And the book the reading plan had me in was Jeremiah.

I did not choose it. I want to be clear about that, because when you read what is on those pages it can look like I saw something coming. I did not see anything coming. The plan had walked me into the back half of Jeremiah months before there was anything on Maui to grieve, and I had read those chapters in whatever ordinary frame of mind you read the Old Testament in on a normal week, and then the fire came, and then I sat down in the aftermath to write the entries I owed. Which means I read Jeremiah twice. Once with the eyes I had before, and once with the eyes I had after. And the second reading is the one that is in the notebook.

Jeremiah is writing to a people who have lost everything. Their city is gone. They are scattered into a land that is not theirs, carrying whatever they could carry, grieving a home that no longer exists. And into that, God gives the prophet a word that is almost offensive in how practical it is.

*From my journal, filed under July 2:*

"S- This captivity is long. Build houses and dwell in them. Plant gardens and eat their fruit. Jer 29:28. O- God was telling the exiled, build homes in the land you have been scattered in. Plant gardens. Make shelters and a food source. The rebuild will take time. A- Lahaina people are scattered. What are their skill sets? What can they do? What is the demand on Maui? What is the natural resources? We need farmers. Entrepreneurs. Government officials. P- God, help me to be a blessing to our community."

Read that again and notice what God does not say. He does not say, I will bring you home next week. He does not tell the exiles to keep their bags packed and their shoes on and to sit by the road watching for the rescue. He tells them to build houses. To plant gardens. To settle into the very place of their loss and begin the slow work of making a life there, because — and this is the hard mercy of it — the captivity is long. The rebuild will take time.

I needed to hear that in September, and I think a whole island needed to hear it, though most of the island was not reading Jeremiah. Everyone wanted the fast answer. I wanted the fast answer. When a thing that terrible happens, every cell in you wants the clock to jump forward to the part where it is fixed. And God, through the prophet, will not let you have it. He hands you a shovel instead. Plant something. Build something. This is going to take longer than your grief wants it to take, and I am asking you to live faithfully inside the length of it. You can hear in my application that my mind had already jumped to it — farmers, entrepreneurs, government officials, what can these scattered people do, what does the island need — and I did not know yet that some of those very questions would one day take shape as something planted at scale. That is a later chapter. For now it was just a man in September, reading a word about the long rebuild, and starting to believe the length of it was not God's absence but God's plan.

The next day the reading turned toward the ones who would have to carry it.

*From my journal, filed under July 3:*

"S- I will satiate the soul of the priests with abundance, and my people shall be satisfied with my goodness says the Lord. Jer 31:14. O- The priests are not neglected by the Lord but we must empower all believers for the work of the ministry. A- God help me to be a blessing to our Lahaina community."

I read that one as a promise made to the tired. The priests in that verse are the ones doing the work, the ones spending themselves on everybody else, and God stops in the middle of the passage to say He will satisfy their souls too. I would not understand for many months how much I needed that particular promise, or how empty my own soul was going to get before I let anyone fill it. That reckoning is further down the road, and I will tell it when I get there. But it is worth noticing that the word was already on the page in July, filed in September, waiting for me to be honest enough to need it. The Lord does not neglect the priests. He satiates them with abundance. I wrote the verse down long before I could have told you it was about me.

And then a few days on, an image I have not been able to shake since.

*From my journal, filed under July 12:*

"S- In this place that is desolate, without man and without beast and in all its cities, there shall be again a dwelling place of shepherds causing their flocks to lie down. Jer 33:12. O- Desolation of a city gives an opportunity for shepherds to find space to lead. Both good shepherds and evil. A- Good shepherds give rest to their sheep. Ps 23. The good shepherd makes to lie down in green pastures, still waters. Restores the soul. P- Lord, help me to be a good shepherd to the displaced residents of Lahaina."

A desolate place, God says, with nobody left in it, will become again a place where shepherds make their flocks lie down. Where I sat in September, the desolate place was not a metaphor. I had watched the video. I had smelled the smoke settle over the island. And here was the promise, that a burned and empty ground is exactly the kind of ground where shepherding begins again. I wrote a warning to myself in the observation, that desolation opens space for shepherds both good and evil, because a wound draws all kinds, and some come to heal and some come to feed on the hurting. I had already seen a little of both. But the prayer underneath it was plain. Make me a good shepherd to people who have no pasture left. Make me the kind that leads them to lie down. That is all I knew to ask.

There is one more entry I want to give you, and it does not come from Jeremiah at all. It comes a couple of weeks later, from Lamentations, from the prophet who wrote the ruin of his city into a book of grief and could not stop weeping through it. And right in the middle of that book, in the middle of the worst of it, something turns.

*From my journal, filed under August 1:*

"S- This I recall to my mind therefore have I hope. It is of the Lord's mercy that we are not consumed because His compassions fail not. They are new every morning. Great is thy faithfulness. Lam 3:21. O- Even in the deepest depression, in the devastation of the Exile, being witness to his people's loss of land, liberty, loved ones, national identity, he voices his pain and attributes them to God, but realizes that it is the mercy of God that they haven't been completely consumed. A- In every situation, no matter how bad, God's mercy exists. When I focus on His mercy, He can heal me. P- God, you allowed Maui to be broken, but you have shown your mercy in allowing us to not be completely destroyed. Please help us to be a blessing to those in Lahaina."

Notice the man does not pretend. He voices his pain and he lays it right at God's feet, does not soften it, does not clean it up. The land is gone, the liberty is gone, the loved ones are gone, the whole identity of a people is gone, and he says so. And then, out of that, he turns his mind on purpose — this I recall to my mind, therefore have I hope — to the one fact that survived the fire. It is of the Lord's mercy that we are not consumed. Not that the loss was small. Not that it did not happen. But that it did not finish us. That there is a mercy underneath the ash new every morning, and it was new that morning too.

I wrote that entry on the first of August, which if you know the dates means I wrote it just days before the anniversary of everything, though of course when I filed it I was somewhere in the fall, catching up. The sequence is strange like that. It always is when I fall behind. But the sequence held. It always does, if you refuse to skip.

I have thought many times about what it means that God walked me through Jeremiah and Lamentations in the exact season He knew was coming, and let me read them once innocent and once wounded. I do not think it makes me a prophet. I did not choose the book and I did not know the fire was on its way. I think it means something simpler and better than prophecy. It means that when I could not keep up, when the mornings were swallowed and my hands did not work, the rope I had tied in the calm weather was still knotted to my waist. The reading plan was the rope. The discipline was the rope. And on the far side of the storm, when I finally sat down to catch up, the words that were waiting for me on those undated pages were exactly the words a scattered people are given: build houses, plant gardens, the rebuild will take time, the shepherds will lead again in the desolate place, and the mercy that kept you from being consumed is new this morning and will be new tomorrow.

I did not have to invent any of it. I only had to catch up.

Chapter 4
No Permission to Grieve

There is a grief that waits.

It does not come when the thing happens. It stands off to the side and lets everyone else go first, because it has decided, without ever saying so out loud, that it has no right to be in the room. It looks around at all the people who lost more, and it steps back. It tells itself that its own loss is small, that the others have earned their tears and it has not, that the honest thing to do is to keep helping and let the ones who really suffered do the weeping. And so it waits. Sometimes for months. Sometimes longer than that.

I did not have a name for that grief until my daughter taught me one.

I have told you already about those first months, the sanctuary turned into a shelter, the sitting with people in their sorrow for three and four months at a stretch. My daughter was in the middle of all of it with us. She is a Maui kid. Lahaina was not a news story to her any more than it was to me. It was part of her childhood and part of her adolescence, the way a place gets woven into a young person's life without them ever deciding to let it in. She had her proms there. She went to the beach there with her friends, over and over, the way you do when you are young and the water is close and the afternoon is long. She shopped there with family. She ate at the restaurants she loved. When I say Lahaina was hers, I do not mean she owned a piece of it. I mean it owned a piece of her, the piece where a person keeps the ordinary good days that a life is actually built out of.

And when it burned, she did not cry.

She worked instead. She was surrounded, all summer, by people who had lost everything. People who had lived in Lahaina their whole lives. People who worked there, whose jobs and homes and family members were gone in a single night. Next to a loss like that, what was hers? A place she liked to go on the weekend. Some memories. She looked at the magnitude of what the people around her were carrying, and she made the calculation that grief makes when it decides it is not welcome. She told herself she had no right. Who was she to weep over restaurants and proms when the person beside her had buried somebody? So she folded her own loss up small and put it away and kept helping, because helping was allowed and grieving was not.

She felt no permission to grieve.

I want to sit on that phrase, because I think it is one of the quiet epidemics of a disaster, and almost nobody counts it. When something terrible happens to a community, everyone measures. They measure without meaning to. They line their loss up against the loss next to them and they rank it, and if it comes out smaller they disqualify it. The man whose house survived will not grieve in front of the man whose house is gone. The volunteer will not grieve in front of the victim. The one who lost a home will not grieve in front of the one who lost a person. Everybody defers upward to a bigger sorrow, and because everybody defers, a whole layer of grief never gets grieved at all. It just gets swallowed and carried, quietly, by people who have decided they are the helpers and not the hurt.

My daughter carried hers all the way to the mainland.

When summer ended she had to fly up to Spokane. She was starting school, thousands of miles from the island, from the smoke, from the people she had spent all those months serving. And at the school there was a counselor who did a simple thing. The counselor called together anyone from Hawaii, any student who had come from the islands, to sit in a peer support group and talk about the fires. Just a circle of people who had come from the same wounded place, in a city where nobody else could really understand what they had left behind.

And in that circle, for the first time since Lahaina burned, my daughter cried. Not the way you cry at a sad movie. She cried uncontrollably. It came up out of her from somewhere she had kept sealed all summer, and it would not stop.

When she told me about it afterward, she had figured out why. It was not the group that broke something open in her, exactly. It was that for the first time she was somewhere she had permission. Up in Spokane she was no longer the smallest loss in the room. She was, in that circle, the one who had been closest to the disaster. The others had watched it from far away. She had lived at the edge of it, had been in the middle of the response, had smelled the smoke settle over her own island. And so, for the first time, people turned to her and asked how she was doing. Not how the victims were doing. Not how the relief was going. How she was. And the moment somebody asked, the grief she had been refusing all summer finally understood it was allowed, and it came.

She grieved a town she loved. She let herself, at last, lose Lahaina — the proms and the beach and the restaurants and the whole geography of her growing up. She had lost it in August with everyone else. But she did not get to feel it until September, in a college counselor's office two thousand miles away, when someone gave her the permission she had refused to give herself.

I have thought about that circle in Spokane many times. Because my daughter is not unusual. She is the rule. All over Maui there were people exactly like her, standing one rung down from a bigger grief and deciding that meant they had no grief of their own. Some of them were in my church. Some of them opened their homes and gave away everything they had and never once sat down to mourn what they themselves had lost, because they were too busy tending losses they judged to be larger. A whole island of helpers, and helping had become the reason none of them wept. You cannot grieve and carry a truckload of supplies at the same time. You cannot grieve and hand a family the keys to your spare room. So the grief waited, the way it always waits, standing off to the side, telling itself it had no right to be in the room.

I know this is true because it happened to me.

It happened later than it happened to my daughter — many months later, and in a very different room. And I am not going to tell you the whole of that here, because it belongs to the next part of this book, and it deserves to be told slowly and in its right place. But I will name it now, plainly, because a man who is writing a chapter called "No Permission to Grieve" has no business pretending he was the exception. I was not the exception. I was the clearest case of it I know.

I was the pastor. I was the one who opened the church, who wrote the checks, who slept on the floor for security, who sat with the families in their sorrow and prayed and did the next thing and then the next thing after that. And a pastor, of all people, tells himself he has no permission. Everyone in front of me had lost more than I had. That was simply true. My house did not burn. I did not have to bury anyone that night. Next to the four-tragedy family, next to the widows I will tell you about, next to the person who lost a mother or a father or a child, what claim did I have on grief? None that I could see. So I did the same thing my daughter did. I folded it up small and put it away and kept working, and I told myself that was strength.

It was not strength. It was a grief that was waiting. And it waited a long time, and it cost me more than I knew, and the day it finally caught up with me I discovered that something in me had broken while I wasn't looking. That is the story of the next chapter, and I will not get ahead of it.

But I want to leave you here with the thing my daughter taught me, because it is smaller than the theology I have promised you and just as important. Grief is not a competition. There is no line, and there is no rank, and there is no rule that says the person who lost more gets to weep and the person who lost less has to stay dry-eyed and useful. That rule is a lie, and it is a cruel one, because it does its worst work on the most faithful people — on the helpers, the servants, the ones who show up. It convinces exactly the people who are pouring themselves out that they are the ones with no right to be poured into.

Even Jesus, standing outside a grave He was about to open, standing there knowing He would raise the dead man back to life inside the hour, did not tell Himself He had no permission. He wept. The shortest verse in the Bible is the sound of God giving Himself leave to grieve a loss He was already about to reverse. If He wept at a grave He was about to empty, then no one who is still standing in the smoke has to pretend they are fine.

My daughter did not get her permission from a sermon. She got it from a stranger in a circle in Spokane who turned to her and asked how she was doing. Sometimes that is all it takes. Sometimes the whole gift you can give a person carrying a hidden grief is to stop asking about the disaster and ask about them. To look at the helper and see, underneath the help, a person who lost something too, and who has been waiting all this time for someone to notice.

She waited until September. I waited longer than that. But the grief was never going to disappear because we refused to feel it. It was only going to wait. It always waits. And it does not go away until you let it come.

Chapter 5
The Night the Oil Ran Dry

Jesus told a story once about ten young women waiting for a bridegroom.

They all had lamps and they were all waiting for the same thing. Five were wise and five were foolish, and the only difference between the two groups — the whole difference, the entire point of the parable — was oil. The wise ones brought extra. The foolish ones brought only what was already burning in the lamp. Then the bridegroom was delayed, longer than any of them had planned for, and while they waited the lamps burned low, and at midnight, when the cry finally went up that he was coming, the foolish women looked down and saw their flames going out and had nothing left to pour in.

I have read that parable my whole life and always thought it was about being ready. It is. But there is something underneath the readiness that I did not see until Lahaina, and it is this: the danger is not the fire. The danger is the length of the wait. The foolish women did not fail because a crisis came. They failed because the crisis lasted longer than their supply. They ran out in the middle of the night, and there is no store open in the middle of the night, and you cannot borrow oil from someone else, because in a season like that everyone is running their own lamp down to the wick.

Nobody warns you about the oil when the fire starts.

At the start there is so much of it you cannot imagine ever running low. In those first days I told you about, the truckloads and the checks and the sanctuary floor full of sleeping families, I was not tired. I want to be honest about that, because it is the part that fools you. Crisis comes with its own strange fuel. The adrenaline is real, and the purpose is real, and you get up every morning knowing exactly what to do and why it matters, and there is a kind of terrible clarity in it that feels almost like strength. You are not counting the oil because the lamp is burning bright and the flame is high and the work is holy and you cannot conceive of the night lasting long enough to matter.

But the night lasts.

There is a language for what happens to a community after a disaster, and I learned it the way you learn any language, by living inside it. Rescue. Relief. Recovery. Those are the stages, and they do not go by in a week. Rescue is the frantic part, the part the cameras come for, the part where you are pulling people toward safety and getting water and gas and medicine to the ones running toward the flames. Then relief, the long feeding and clothing and sheltering of people who have nothing, which does not end when the news trucks leave. Then recovery, which is the longest of all, the slow grinding forward through insurance and housing and grief and the ten thousand small ruined things that have to be rebuilt one at a time. You do not move through those stages in order and then rest. You live in all of them at once, for six months, for twelve, on high tilt the entire time, and the tilt becomes so normal that you forget a person is not built to stand at that angle.

And through the whole of it, there were the funerals.

There were a great many of them, in those months. People lost family in that fire, and the living had to bury them, and a pastor is one of the people who stands up front when that happens. I did that work over and over across that year, and I would not trade having done it, because it is one of the truest things a pastor is ever given to do, to stand with a family in the worst hour of their lives and speak the name of the God who holds the dead. But there is a cost to it that accrues quietly, one service at a time, and I was not counting the cost any more than I was counting the oil.

I did not see what it was doing to me until a single moment at a single funeral.

I was standing there talking with someone who had just lost their father. And I noticed, as if from somewhere outside myself, the tone that was coming out of my mouth. I was almost joking with them. Light. Easy. The way you talk to somebody at a party, at a barbecue, at some happy gathering where nothing is at stake. This was a grave, and a grieving child, and I was chatting like it was a good afternoon. And even as it was happening I felt a cold drop of recognition go through me, because that is not who I am and that is not how I hold the grieving. Something had gone numb. Something had worn through. I stood at that funeral making light conversation over a fresh loss, and I understood, quietly and completely, that something in me was broken.

I did not have a name for it that day. I found the name later, and the name is compassion fatigue. It is what happens to helpers, to nurses and chaplains and first responders and pastors, when they pour out compassion faster than it can be replenished, day after day, with no time to refill. The well does not announce that it is going dry. You keep lowering the bucket and one day it comes up with less, and then less than that, and then one afternoon you catch yourself laughing at a graveside and realize the bucket is scraping the bottom. The oil in the lamp is nearly gone, and you are still standing there in the dark expecting it to burn.

There was something else riding underneath it that I could not name for a long while either, and it was a strange kind of guilt. My house did not burn. I told you already, in the chapter before this one, that I gave myself no permission to grieve because everyone in front of me had lost more than I had, and that was true, and it was its own trap. But there was a second thing braided into it. There is a particular ache that belongs to the ones who came through, the ones who still have their homes and their families intact, standing in front of people who lost everything. Survival guilt, they call it. You feel that your wholeness is somehow an offense in the presence of their ruin. So you work harder, to earn the standing you have no way to earn, and you drive the lamp lower, and you tell yourself that the exhaustion is the least you owe.

That is the condition I carried into a retreat, many months after the fire.

It was a gathering of pastors — men who had been doing what I had been doing, standing where I had been standing, all across that same wounded island. Somebody had the wisdom to build it around rest. Rest, review, recalibrate, reconnect. To reconnect with God, and with our wives, and with each other. And to sit down in a room together and finally say out loud, to people who would understand it, what the year had actually cost us.

I remember a piece of it that has stayed with me. Someone asked what advice we would give to a pastor six months out from a disaster, and the answer that came back was not soft. Buckle up. It is not over. Build deep with God and with others. That was it. Not a promise that the hard part had passed, but a warning that it had not, and a single instruction for surviving what remained: go deep, with God and with the people around you, because shallow will not hold under this weight. We talked about staff spread thin. About managing what little energy we had left. About marriages and friendships strained by the tilt. About survival guilt and compassion fatigue, the two things I had been carrying without a name, spoken plainly by other men who were carrying them too.

And in that room, among those pastors, I broke down.

I had spent the whole year as the one who held other people while they wept. I had stood at the funerals and sat on the sanctuary floor and prayed with the ones whose losses were larger than mine, and I had never once let it be about me, because I did not believe it was allowed to be. But in that room it finally was. These were not people I was serving. They were people who knew, from the inside, exactly what I had been through, and there was nothing left in front of me to protect them from. So I said it — that I was struggling, that something in me was broken, that I needed help myself — and it came apart, and I wept, and the room held me the way I had spent a year holding everyone else. It was healing, and not only for me. It was healing for all of us, because every man in that room had been running his lamp down to the wick and telling himself he was fine.

That was the night I understood the parable differently.

The ten virgins were not condemned for waiting. They were all waiting; that was the whole assignment. They were divided by oil. And I had been living like one of the foolish ones, burning what was in the lamp and bringing nothing extra, because the crisis felt too urgent to stop and refill. But the extra oil is not a luxury. The extra oil is the point. You have to be prayed up, filled up, rested up, before the long night, because you cannot manufacture any of those things at midnight when the flame is already guttering. I had learned it the hard way. I had run out of oil at a graveside and heard it in my own voice.

There was a line I wrote down in those days that I have carried ever since. Three short pieces of it. Life is beautiful. Lahaina is gone. Everything is temporary. I set them side by side because they are all true at the same time, and you have to hold all three or you will go mad holding only one. Life is beautiful — you cannot let the fire talk you out of that, and there is beauty even in the ash, even at the graveside, even in a room full of broken pastors weeping. Lahaina is gone — you cannot pretend otherwise, cannot spiritualize the loss into something smaller than it was, cannot skip the grief to get to the comfort. And everything is temporary — the town, the pain, the tilt, the very oil in the lamp, all of it passing through your hands and none of it staying. Don't blink, I wrote, because a life is short and a beautiful thing can be gone in a night, and I had watched a hundred-year-old town go in a night, and I did not want to sleepwalk through whatever was left.

I came off that retreat knowing I needed rest, and knowing something more than that. I was beginning to understand, though I could not yet have explained it to you, that the pain I had been trying to outwork was not something to be cured or solved or hurried past. There was a truth waiting on the far side of that exhaustion about what God actually does with our brokenness, and I was only at the edge of it. That truth belongs to a later chapter, and I will get there.

But I want to end this one where the parable ends, in the dark, at the sound of the cry. The foolish women went off to buy oil and missed the very thing they had waited all night for. The wise ones had brought enough to last the length of the wait. I do not tell you this as a man who was wise. I tell it to you as one of the foolish ones who ran dry at midnight and was carried, by the mercy of God and the arms of other broken men, back to a place where the lamp could be filled again. The oil ran out. That is the honest name of that season. But the night was not the end of me, and the running dry turned out to be the beginning of learning where the oil comes from in the first place.

I had been carrying far too much of it alone. I was about to find out, in a room with my wife, just how much.

Chapter 6
Tears I Had Only Seen Twice

The retreat gave me one more thing I have not told you about yet.

Along with the rest and the review, along with the room full of tired pastors finally saying out loud what the year had cost them, there was an offer that seemed small at the time. Free counseling. Not only for the pastors but for our wives. Joce came with me. We sat down together in a quiet room in front of a counselor, and I did the thing I had spent a whole year refusing to do, which was to open my mouth and let out what was actually inside me. Not the sermon version. Not the report from the field. The real weight of it, the funerals and the tilt and the numbness I had caught in my own voice at a graveside, the whole of what I had been carrying since the morning the video came.

And as I was talking, my wife began to cry.

I need to tell you something about Joce for that to land the way it landed on me. She is not a woman who cries easily. She is even. She is steady. In all our years I could count on one hand the number of times I had seen her weep, and I would not have needed all the fingers. The first time was at our wedding, when her father walked her down and gave her to me. The second time was at her father's funeral, when the same man who had given her away was gone. That was the whole list. Two times. A joining and a parting, the two hinges a life turns on, and both of them had to do with her father. That is how rare it was. I had been married to this woman for years and I had watched her cry exactly twice.

And now, in that counseling room, I was watching it a third time.

I did not understand it at first. My mind went where a husband's mind goes. I thought I had done something. I thought maybe, in saying all of it out loud, I had hurt her somehow, dropped a weight on her she had not agreed to carry. Or I thought perhaps this was not about me at all, that she was going through something of her own that the room had opened up, some private grief that had chosen that moment to surface. Those were the two explanations I reached for, because they were the only two I knew how to reach for. It did not occur to me that she might be crying for me. That possibility was not in my vocabulary. I had spent so long being the one who held the weeping that I had genuinely forgotten a person could weep on my account.

But that is what it was.

She told me, when I asked. She said she had not known what I was going through. All that year I had come home and set nothing down. I had walked back into my own house from funeral after funeral, from the sanctuary floor, from the long grinding recovery, and I had told her almost none of it. So she had watched her husband move through the worst season of his life with the door shut, and she had not been able to see inside, and now, in that room, the door came open and she saw the whole of it at once. And it broke her. She said she felt broken for me. Not hurt by me. Not caught up in something of her own. Broken on my behalf, because she was finally seeing what I had been carrying and she loved me, and love that finally sees a hidden wound cannot help but bleed a little for it.

I cannot fully explain why that healed me, but it did. Deeply. There is something in being cried for that goes to a place nothing else reaches. I had spent a year being useful. I had been the steady one, the one with the checks and the plan and the calm voice at the front of the room, and being useful is its own kind of armor, because as long as you are the one helping you never have to be the one helped. But you cannot be cried for and stay in your armor. When my wife wept for me, she was not fixing anything. There was nothing in that moment to fix. She was simply seeing me, the way my daughter finally got to be seen in that circle in Spokane, the way a hidden grief waits its whole life to be seen. And being seen by the person who knows you best, and watching that person's care spill over into the tears she almost never cries — that undid something in me that all my usefulness had been holding shut.

Which brought me to a question I had never really asked. Why had I told her so little?

I have thought about it a great deal since, because I do not think I am unusual among pastors, and I do not think pastors are unusual among people who spend their lives carrying other people. There were reasons, and they were not bad reasons. That is the trap of it. Every one of them was a reason I could defend.

The first was protection. Ministry hands you heavy things, and my instinct, always, was to keep those things away from my wife. Why should she carry the weight of a stranger's tragedy? Why bring the funeral home? Why lay across her shoulders the burdens that were mine to bear as her husband and hers to be spared as my wife? I told myself I was shielding her. And there is love in that, real love, the love of a man who does not want the woman he married to be crushed under the things he sees. But shielding someone from your burdens is not the same as sharing your life with them, and I had confused the two.

The second reason was that I did not want to relive it. Some of what I had seen and sat with over that year, I did not want to walk through twice. To tell it is to live it again, to put yourself back at the graveside, back in the smoke, back in the room where the worst thing was happening. So I kept quiet, not to protect her that time but to protect myself, to keep from paying the cost of the memory a second time in the telling.

And the third reason was the simplest and the most binding. Confidentiality. So much of what a pastor holds is not his to repeat. People tell you the things they tell no one else. They trust you with what they have hidden from the whole world, and you carry it, and you take it home, and you cannot set it down on the kitchen table because it was never yours to hand around. So it stays inside you. You hold it alone because holding it alone is the very thing that was asked of you.

All three of those are real, and I would not throw any of them away. There are burdens a pastor should not lay on his spouse, memories not worth reliving, confidences that must be kept to the grave. But sitting in that room, watching my wife cry the third time in all the years I had known her, I saw that I had let those three good reasons harden into a wall I did not need. Somewhere behind the protection and the not-reliving and the confidentiality, I had walled off things that had nothing to do with any of them. I had walled off myself. I had made a habit of being alone with my own weight until being alone with it felt like faithfulness instead of what it actually was, which was a slow starving. And the plainest sentence I came away with from that whole retreat, the one I keep returning to, is this. I was carrying many things alone that I did not need to be carrying alone.

Not everything. Some things a shepherd carries alone because that is the job. But many things. More than I knew. Things I had filed under protection or confidentiality that were really just filed under pride, or under the quiet fear that if I let anyone all the way in they would see how empty the well had gotten.

There was a page from that season with four words on it that I have come back to more than once. Solitude. Support. Silence. Stress. And underneath them, arrows pointing down — quiet time and rest, connection with God and others. I think I had understood all my life how to be in solitude and I understood, all too well, how to live under stress. What I had lost, somewhere in the tilt of that year, was the middle. Support. Connection with God and others. Silence that is filled with God rather than emptied by exhaustion. I had all the stress and all the solitude and almost none of the support, and a man built that way will run dry every time, and I had.

And there was one more thing on that page, written small, that I want to end on, because it is the quiet undertone under everything in this chapter and I do not want to dress it up or build a scene around it that did not happen. Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy on me. That prayer was on the page as a tool, a thing to breathe in the middle of stress when there are no other words left. But it was not new to me and it was not born in that retreat. I had been praying for mercy long before the fire — months before, in an ordinary season, asking God for mercy on me and on my kids and on my kids' kids, working it into my prayers through the winter before Lahaina had ever burned. I did not know then why I was asking. I only know now that the word was already in my mouth before I had any idea how much I would need it. The mercy that Lamentations says is new every morning — I had been reaching for it before the morning came that would require it of me.

I think that is often how God works, further ahead of us than we ever realize. He put Jeremiah in my reading plan before there was anything to grieve. He put a prayer for mercy on my lips before there was anything to be spared from. And He put a steady, unweeping woman beside me for years, so that on the day I finally opened the door and let her see, her tears would mean everything — the third time in all our life together, and the one that healed me.

I walked out of that room still tired. Rest was still coming. But I was not alone in the same way anymore, and I have not been since. I had spent a year believing that carrying it alone was the price of the calling. It was not. Some of it was mine to carry, and I would carry it again. But the wall I had built out of good reasons had walled me off from the very people God had given me to be carried by. My wife had known how to weep all along. She had only been waiting, like all of us, for someone to open the door and let her in.

Chapter 7
The Banyan Tree

I promised you in the first chapter that I would come back to the banyan tree and tell it properly. This is the chapter for it, because everything this book is trying to say is written into that one tree, in a language older than any sermon I could preach.

It stood down by the harbor, and it had stood there for more than a hundred years. If you have ever been to Lahaina you have stood under it, whether or not you knew its name, because it was one of those landmarks that a place organizes itself around. It was where the tourists took their photographs. It was where a whole town's worth of ordinary afternoons happened — the shade you ducked into on a hot day, the spot you told a friend to meet you at, the backdrop of a thousand family pictures that are now the only pictures some families have left. A tree like that stops being scenery after a hundred years. It becomes a member of the community. It becomes something the town leans on without ever thinking about it, the way you lean on a wall you have never once looked at.

When the fire came through, the banyan burned like everything else.

It went black. Every leaf gone, every branch scorched, the great trunk standing there in the ash like a monument to what the fire had done to the whole town around it. And in the weeks after, people would go and look at it and ask the question out loud that they were really asking about everything. Is it dead. Is there anything left in there. Can something that burned that badly ever come back, or is this just the shape of what used to be alive, standing up out of the ruin to remind us of what we lost. Nobody knew. It looked finished. Most things that looked like that were finished. You could not have blamed anyone for writing it off, because writing things off was the only honest arithmetic available in those weeks. You looked at what the fire had touched and you assumed it was gone, because almost everything the fire had touched was gone.

Then, months later, the first green shoots appeared.

They came out of the black wood while everything around them was still dry and scorched, and the whole town understood what it meant before a single person had to say it. The roots. Over all those years, in all the ordinary seasons nobody was watching, the roots had gone down deep enough to reach water the tree could not see and the fire could not touch. Everything above ground had burned. Everything around it was ash. And down in the dark, where no flame could get to it, the tree had been holding onto a source the entire time. When the drought and the fire had taken everything a tree is supposed to need, it turned out the banyan had been drinking from something deeper than the weather. And so it lived. Blackened, and scarred, and alive.

To Lahaina it became a symbol of hope, and I understand why. If that tree could rise out of the ashes, maybe the town could too. That is a good and true thing for a town to believe, and I believe it with them. But I want to tell you what the tree came to mean to me, standing there in the aftermath with a prayer already written in my notebook that I did not yet fully understand, because the banyan preached me a sermon I had been reading in Isaiah my whole life and had never once seen standing up in living wood.

Isaiah wrote that God would give beauty for ashes, and the oil of joy for mourning, and the garment of praise for the spirit of heaviness, and that the people this was done to would be called trees of righteousness, the planting of the Lord, that He might be glorified. I have quoted that passage to you already, and I quoted it to myself in the notebook in September when I asked God to let me see the beauty past the ashes. But quoting it and understanding it are two different things, and it took a burned tree to move it the last few inches from my head to my chest.

The whole passage turns on one small word, and the word is for. Beauty for ashes. Joy for mourning. Praise for heaviness. He does not say He will make the ashes disappear. He does not say He will reach down and erase the mourning as though it never happened, or lift the heaviness off you and throw it away where you will never have to see it again. He says He will give you something for it. He will trade. It is an exchange, and an exchange only works if both sides have something the other one wants.

Which means — and I want you to sit with this, because it took me a long time to sit with it — the ashes have value to God.

Your mourning has value. Your heaviness has value. The very things you are most ashamed of carrying, the grief you have folded up small because you decided you had no right to it, the pain you would give anything to be rid of — God is not asking you to throw those things away. He is asking for them. He wants them. He looks at the ash of your life the way I looked at that blackened tree, and where you see only what the fire took, He sees the raw material of an exchange He is longing to make with you. The ashes are not the obstacle to the beauty. The ashes are the price of it. They are what He is asking you to bring.

That runs so hard against the grain of everything we are taught that most of us cannot hear it the first time. Because what does the world tell you to do with pain? Medicate it. Hide it. Cure it. Solve it. Get rid of it as fast as you possibly can, by whatever means are nearest to hand, and count yourself healed on the day you finally stop feeling it. We treat pain like a poison to be flushed out of the system, and we treat the person who is still feeling theirs, months or years later, as someone who has failed to complete the process. Just heal. Just move on. Just get past it. As though the goal of a wounded life were to arrive as quickly as possible back at the place where nothing hurts.

I believed a version of that myself, and I would have gone on believing it, except that I learned something in grief counseling that broke the whole framework apart. I learned that trauma never actually heals.

Let me say that carefully, because it sounds like despair and it is the opposite of despair. Trauma does not heal the way a cut heals, closing over until you cannot find where it was. What trauma does is change the depth at which you feel. It reaches down into you and lowers the floor, and after that you simply feel everything from further down than you used to. The person who has been through it is not broken in a way that will one day be repaired back to factory condition. They are changed. They feel deeper now. And here is the part that turned me all the way around: the same receptors in us that let us feel that depth of pain are the very receptors that let us feel joy. It is one instrument, not two. The studies bear it out — the people who have lived with the most joy in this life are, over and over, the same people who have felt the greatest pain. The capacity is the same capacity. You do not get one channel for sorrow and a separate channel for gladness. You get one depth, and everything comes through it.

Which means when you medicate the pain away, you are not performing a clean surgery that removes the bad and leaves the good. You are cutting the nerve. And in exactly the same measure that you numb yourself to the pain, you go numb to the joy. You cannot deaden one end of the instrument and keep the other end ringing. The person who succeeds in never feeling their grief has also, without meaning to, arranged never to feel much of anything, and they will spend the rest of their life wondering why the good things do not reach them the way they seem to reach other people. They amputated the very thing that would have let them feel it. They got rid of the ashes, and in getting rid of the ashes they threw away the only thing they had that God was asking to exchange.

When I understood that, I stopped running from the pain. I did not learn to enjoy it — you do not learn to enjoy it, and anyone who tells you they have is selling something. But I stopped treating it as an enemy to be defeated and started treating it as something to be embraced and then handed over. Embrace it, and exchange it. That is the whole movement of this book in three words. You feel it all the way down, because feeling it all the way down is what keeps the instrument alive. And then you do not keep it, and you do not medicate it, and you do not build a wall of good reasons around it the way I did. You bring it to God. You put the ashes in His hands, and you let Him make the trade only He can make, which is to give you back, in the very same measure, a capacity for joy and love and grace as deep as the pain that carved the well.

That is what the banyan was doing all along, and none of us could see it. While the town wondered whether it was dead, it was drinking. The fire took everything on the surface, everything that was for show, everything the photographs were of — and the tree just kept quietly reaching down to the source the fire could not reach, and turned all that scorched ruin into the platform for new green. It did not pretend it had not burned. You can still see the black on it. The scars are part of the tree now. But the scars did not stop the life; the depth of the roots outlasted the reach of the flame, and the tree came back not in spite of what it had suffered but with what it had suffered still written all over it, alive and glorified.

There is a story from the night of the fire that belongs in this chapter, in the territory of green shoots and things that should not have survived, and I am going to set it down plainly and not one word further than I know. There was a woman who walked into a bathroom during the fire and prayed there through the night. When the morning came, that bathroom was the only structure left standing. I know that much and I know there are photographs of it, and I am not going to dress it up, because it does not need dressing up and it is not mine to embellish.

*[FOR THE INTERVIEW: The bathroom that stood — who is she and her relationship to the church, where was the bathroom, why was she there during the fire, her own words about that night, when she learned it was the only structure standing, and what happened to her afterward?]*

I only put it here because it belongs to the same truth as the tree. All around it, everything burned. And down in the middle of the black, someone was holding onto a source the fire could not touch, and in the morning there was something left standing that had no business being there.

That is the exchange. God does not promise you that the fire will not come, and He does not promise to give you back the town you lost, board for board, the way it was. Lahaina is gone; I have not stopped telling you that and I will not start pretending otherwise. But He promises that if you will bring Him the ashes instead of hiding them, He will trade you for them. Beauty for ashes. Joy for mourning. Praise for heaviness. Trees of righteousness, the planting of the Lord, blackened and scarred and unmistakably alive, standing up out of the ruin with the fire still written on them, so that everyone who walks past has to ask the same question the whole town asked about that tree — how is this thing still living — and has to arrive, whether they want to or not, at the only answer there is.

The roots went down deeper than the fire could reach. They always had. We just could not see it until everything on the surface was gone.

Chapter 8
The Widows

There were two widows in our church, and for years I thought I knew what they were.

I thought they were the ones I was there to help. That is not a small thing, and I do not want to make it sound like one. A pastor spends a great deal of his life bending toward the people who have lost the most, and these two women had lost as much as anyone I knew. I carried them in my prayers the way you carry the wounded. When I thought of them, I felt compassion, and underneath the compassion I filed them, without ever meaning to, under a single word. Victims. It is the word that comes most naturally to a helper, because it tells him what his job is. Here is a person to whom a terrible thing happened. Stand near them. Be gentle. Do what you can.

I was wrong about the word. Not about the terrible things — those were real, and I will tell them — but about what the terrible things had made. It took a whole town burning down for me to see it, and when I saw it I had to go back and rewrite everything I thought I understood about the two of them, and about pain, and about what God does with the people it breaks.

The first woman lost her husband to the war.

He was a soldier, and it was his last tour in Afghanistan. That is the detail I keep turning over, because of what it does to the rest of the story. It was the last one. They were almost through it. They had made the plans that a couple makes when the hardest part is nearly behind them, the plans about the years they would finally get to spend together, the ordinary long life on the far side of the deployments, the three children they would raise in peace. She was pregnant with the last of those children when he went. And he never came back. The baby she was carrying would be born into a family that had already lost its father, and she would raise all three of them without the man she had made the plans with. There is a particular cruelty in a loss that comes at the very end, when the danger was supposed to be almost over. She lived that cruelty. She built a whole life on the other side of it.

The second loss I was there for myself, and I have never been able to set it down.

There was a man in one of my small groups. He loved to run. That was one of the things you knew about him, the way you know the plain good things about the people in your group — he ran. He had a wife and two young boys. And one night my phone rang at eleven-thirty, and the call was about him, and the strange, awful thing about it was the geography. He was at the bottom of the hill where I live. Right there. A drunk driver had hit him. I drove down my own hill in the dark toward a scene I could almost see from my house, and when I got there his wife was already there, and the police told me what they had to tell me, which was that he had been killed on impact. He had gone out to run, the way he had a thousand times, and he did not come home, and it happened at the foot of the road I drive every day.

Then came the part I would give almost anything not to have had to do. Somebody had to tell his boys. Somebody had to be the one to say to two young children that their father had died, that he was not coming back, that they would not see him again. I was that somebody. I have preached on death for most of my adult life, and I have stood at more gravesides than I can count, and I did not have a single word that was equal to the face of that little boy. He kept asking me why. Just that. Why. And I had no answer. There is no answer. I have never pretended to him or to anyone else that I had one. So I did the only thing that was left to do, which was to hold him and cry with him. That is all I had. A pastor with nothing to say, holding a fatherless boy at the bottom of a hill, weeping, because the question he was asking is the one question the whole world has never been able to answer.

Two women. One whose husband did not come home from a war that was nearly over. One whose husband did not come home from a run down the street. I felt for them both for years, and I would have told you, if you had asked, that these were two of the people in my church who most needed to be carried.

And then Lahaina burned, and I watched them pick up the whole town.

Both of them opened their homes. Not for a night, not as a gesture, but for many nights, to complete strangers — people off the burned coast whom they had never met, whom they owed nothing, who could not have paid them back if they had wanted to. These were not women with an abundance to spare. One of them, the one who lost her husband, took in more than forty people. Let me say that again, because when I first heard it I had to stop and count it out in my head. More than forty. In her home. And she had no husband to share the weight of it, and she had her own children to raise at the same time, and she did it anyway, night after night, a widow running a shelter with her own two hands while the rest of us with our two parents and our two incomes were still trying to figure out where to put people.

I asked them both, at different times, how they could do it. It was not a rhetorical question. I genuinely did not understand where a person finds that, after what they had been through, and I wanted to know. And they gave me, without having compared notes, the same answer. They said: we know what loss is. We have been there. We know what it is like. How can we not be there for these people who are going through the same thing.

How can we not be there.

I have thought about that sentence more than almost anything else anyone said to me in those years, because it turns the whole logic of suffering upside down. The world would tell you that a person who has lost that much has earned the right to keep whatever she has left. That she, of all people, should be excused, should be allowed to protect herself, should be the last one asked to give. She has suffered enough. Let someone with more to spare do the giving. That is how we reckon it. But these two women reckoned it the opposite way, and they were not being noble, and they were not straining after a virtue. They were simply telling me the truth about themselves. The loss had not emptied them. It had opened them. They looked at a family standing on the ash of their own home with the same question in their eyes that a fatherless boy had once asked me at the bottom of a hill, and where I had no answer, these women had a house. They had a couch. They had forty places to sleep. And they could not not give them, because they knew, from the inside, exactly what that family was feeling, and you cannot un-know a thing like that.

That is when I understood I had filed them under the wrong word all along.

They were not victims. Somewhere in the years between their worst nights and the fire, God had been at work in them, down where I could not see it, and He had grown something in the dark that I had completely missed. There was a strength in these two women that most people never come near, and it had not appeared in spite of what they suffered. It had been produced by it. The apostle says it plainly, that tribulation produces — it does not merely survive, it produces — and I had read that a hundred times and never watched it happen in front of me until I watched two widows shelter a town. The suffering had gone down into them like roots into dry ground, and it had reached a source, and the source had made them strong in a way that the rest of us, the unbroken ones, simply were not.

Which is the exchange I told you about in the last chapter, standing up and walking around in the shape of two women. Beauty for ashes. I had explained it as a theology, the small word for, the truth that our ashes have value to God and that He trades us for them. Here it was proven. These two had brought God the worst nights of their lives, the pregnant goodbye and the eleven-thirty phone call, and they had not medicated it away and they had not hidden it and they had not spent the intervening years merely coping. They had given it to Him. And He had given them back, in the very same measure, a capacity to love that most of us never develop, because most of us have never been broken deeply enough to grow it. Their pain had increased their capacity — that is the plainest way I know to say it. The depth the grief carved in them was the exact depth from which the love now poured. They could hold forty strangers because they had once been held by no one but God at the bottom of their own worst night, and they remembered what it was, and they could not stand to let anyone else be there alone.

It taught me something about people that I have not been able to unlearn. From the outside, everyone looks the same. You pass a woman in the grocery store and you have no idea. She looks like anyone. But we are not all the same. Some people are carrying, underneath the ordinary surface, a strength that was forged in a furnace you cannot see, and you would never know it until the day a furnace comes for everyone and you watch who runs toward the fire. When Lahaina burned, I found out who in my church had that hidden strength, and it was not always who I would have guessed. Over and over it was the ones I had been carrying. The ones I had filed under victim. They rose up while I was still reeling, and they carried more than I did, because they had already been where the whole island was now going, and they had come back from it changed.

And here is the part that undid me, quietly, in the middle of all of it. I realized that God was not only doing this in those two women. He was doing it in the whole church. He was doing it in me. The same strength that had been produced in a war widow and a runner's widow was being produced, right then, in a whole community of people who had lost a town — the beginning of something I would only see the full size of much later. The fire was not only destroying. Down where none of us could watch, it was growing something. It was making, out of an island of victims, an island of people who would one day be able to say to the next sufferer the thing my two widows said to me. We know what loss is. How can we not be there.

I had spent years thinking my job was to stand near the broken and be gentle. That is still part of it, and I will do it until I die. But the widows taught me that the broken are not only to be tended. Sometimes they are to be followed. Sometimes the person to learn from in a disaster is not the strong one who arrived unhurt to help. It is the one who was shattered years ago and has been quietly turning the shards into strength ever since, waiting, though none of us knew she was waiting, for the day the rest of us would finally need to know how it is done.

They knew how it was done. They had been to the bottom of the hill. And when the whole island ended up down there in the dark, asking the same unanswerable why, these two women were already there, with the lights on and the doors open, holding whoever came, because someone had once held them, and they had never forgotten what it was worth.

Chapter 9
Praying with the Mayor

I told you back in Jeremiah that when I sat down in September to catch up on the summer, my mind ran ahead of me on the page. I read the prophet telling the exiles to build houses and plant gardens because the rebuild would take time, and I started making a list in the margin of what a scattered people would need. Farmers. Entrepreneurs. Government officials. I wrote those three words down without much thought, the way you jot anything in a notebook nobody is going to read, and then I moved on to the next verse.

I did not know then that God had already put a government official in the story. Or that I would spend the next years of my life, once a month, on my knees in his office.

The mayor of Maui opened his office for prayer after the fires, and we have been going in to pray with him every month since. That sentence is easy to write and I want to slow down on it, because I do not think most people understand how unusual it is. We are not talking about a photograph on the courthouse steps, a bowed head for the cameras and then back to business. We are talking about the man who carries the weight of the whole island's recovery clearing time on his calendar, month after month, with no press and no agenda, to let a group of pastors come in and lift up the county to God. Mayor Bissen did that. He is still doing it. And I have watched what happens in a place when the person at the top of it is willing to be prayed over instead of only prayed about.

There is a verse in that same stretch of Jeremiah I gave you earlier. A desolate city, God said, becomes again a place where shepherds make their flocks lie down — and I wrote in my notebook that desolation opens space for shepherds both good and evil, because a wound draws all kinds. Some come to heal and some come to feed. I had already seen a little of both by the time we started meeting with the mayor. What I had not expected was to find, in the seat of civic power, a man who wanted the good shepherds in the room. Who understood that leading a broken island was not something he could do out of his own supply, and who went looking for extra oil in the only place it is actually kept.

I have prayed for a lot of things in that office. But mostly, over and over, month after month, we have prayed for one thing, and it is the thing I asked God for on the twenty-third of August in 2023, before any of this had a shape. Wisdom. We prayed that Mayor Bissen would lead with wisdom and guidance, that God would give him the kind of discernment Solomon asked for when he stood over a job too big for any one man. And I want to tell you what I have watched come of those prayers, because I kept track. We kept a list. The church kept a running ledger of the things we asked for in that room and the things we then watched happen, and I am going to set it down here plainly, because a list of answered prayers is its own kind of testimony, and this one belongs to a whole island and not to me.

Here is some of what we prayed for, and here is what happened.

- The residential cleanup — the removal of the burned remains of a whole town — finished ahead of schedule. FEMA itself commended it.
- We prayed for the rebuild to begin, and the first house was underway within ten months. In March of 2025 the first homeowners moved back in, in Kula and in Lahaina — real people, back on their own land, with keys in their hands.
- Before the fire, Maui was the county with the highest fentanyl use in the state. We prayed against it. It is now the lowest.
- The toxic waste from the burn zone had to be relocated — and then relocated again, and the federal government paid for the move both times. Anyone who works in disaster recovery will tell you that never happens. It happened here. Twice.
- West Maui is connected to the rest of us by one road through a bottleneck, and everyone braced for the debris trucks to snarl it for hours — two-hour delays were the expectation. There were none. Not the disruption everyone feared.
- Maui was awarded 1.6 billion dollars in recovery funding, the highest award for any disaster.
- The water — long one of the hardest fights on the west side — moved toward cooperation, parties that had been at odds beginning to work together, the county moving to take on the systems.
- Kula and Lahaina were in drought. We prayed for rain. The rain came. On our list somebody wrote next to it only two words: 'Nuf said.
- Construction on the island is up a hundred and twenty-five percent. Unemployment is down. Visitor spending this past January came in at 664.7 million dollars against 534.9 the January before — up almost a quarter.
- And a hundred and seventy-five homes are already occupied. In under three years from the fire.

I could keep going, but you can feel the shape of it. Maui is rebuilding faster than any other natural disaster site in the United States. I do not say that as a boast about our competence, because it is not about our competence. I say it as a report from the prayer room. We asked, month after month, in the mayor's office, and this is what came back. I am a pastor and not a statistician, and I know the world has other explanations ready, and I am not interested in arguing anyone out of theirs. I am only telling you what we prayed and what we then watched. God said through the prophet, call to me and I will answer you and show you great and mighty things which you do not know. I wrote that verse in my journal in September, in my own grief, asking Him to show me the beauty past the ashes. He has been answering it at the scale of a whole county ever since, one commended cleanup, one moved homeowner, one improbable rain at a time.

But I promised you at the start of this book that I would not spiritualize the loss into something smaller than it was, and I am not going to start now, three years in, just because the news has gotten good. A list like that can lie by leaving things out. So let me tell you the other half, the half that does not make a praise report.

The recovery has cost the island things that do not show up on a ledger of victories. When the federal money came in to house the displaced, it came in the form of rents paid at rates far above what anything on Maui had ever cost, and that money, meant as mercy, did what a flood of money always does. It lifted the price of everything it touched. Families who never lost a thing in the fire suddenly could not afford the island they had always lived on, because the going rate for a roof had been pulled up out of their reach by a program designed to help someone else. Housing was scarce before the fire. After it, with a whole town's worth of people needing somewhere to live, it became scarcer than most people outside these islands can imagine. I have watched the recovery of Lahaina put pressure on people in Kahului and Kihei and upcountry who had no direct part in the tragedy and are quietly paying for it anyway. That is true at the same time as everything on that list is true. Both things are the recovery. A mercy and a cost, braided together, the way they always are in a real world that does not sort itself into clean columns.

And there is one more honesty I owe you, and it is harder to write than the costs, because it is not a number.

It is the length.

I told you in the chapter about the oil that the danger in a crisis is never the fire itself but the wait, the way the night lasts longer than anyone packed for. That is as true of a county as it is of a man. The world watched Maui burn. The cameras came for the rescue and stayed a little while into the relief, and then they left, the way they always leave, and the recovery — the longest stage, the grinding years of it — happened almost entirely with no one watching. There is a middle to every disaster that no one narrates. The second year. The stretch after the anniversary specials have aired and before the ribbon-cuttings, when the town is neither burning nor rebuilt, just slowly, unglamorously becoming itself again, house by house, permit by permit, and nobody outside is paying attention anymore. That middle is where most of the actual faithfulness happens. It is also where most of the discouragement lives, because it is very hard to keep believing in a rebuild that no one is applauding, in a season when the answered prayers are real but slow, and the slowness feels, some days, like silence.

I lived in that middle. So did the mayor, and the families back on their lots, and the churches with their feeding kitchens, and everyone on this island who kept showing up long after it stopped being a story. And I want to put one moment from it into this book — a scene from that second year, one of the ordinary unwitnessed days when the rebuild was just work and no one was watching — but that is exactly the kind of moment a man's memory blurs, precisely because nobody was recording it, and I would rather tell it true than dress it up from the fog.

*[FOR THE INTERVIEW: A specific scene from the second year — an ordinary day in the long unwitnessed middle of the recovery (a prayer meeting in the mayor's office, a visit to a rebuild site, a conversation with a family still displaced). What was said, what you saw, what it felt like to keep believing when no one outside was watching anymore?]*

What I can tell you about that middle is that the monthly prayer never stopped, and I think that is the real reason the list on our wall kept growing. It is easy to pray in the fire, when the adrenaline is high and the whole world is leaning in. It is another thing to keep an appointment on the mayor's calendar in the eighteenth month, in the twenty-fourth, when the emergency has cooled into a long slog and there is no drama left to carry you into the room. But we kept coming, and he kept opening the door, and month after month we asked for wisdom over a job too big for any man, and month after month God did what He said He would do, which was to answer and to show us great and mighty things we did not know.

I keep going back to those three words I wrote in the margin in September. Farmers. Entrepreneurs. Government officials. I had read Jeremiah telling a scattered people what they would need to rebuild, and I had made my little list, and I had prayed the same prayer I prayed under every entry that summer — God, help me to be a blessing to our community. I did not understand, writing it, that being a blessing to the community might look like this. Not a truckload of supplies. Not a five-hundred-dollar check. A standing appointment. A bowed head in a room where decisions get made. The slow, unglamorous, faithful work of asking God, over and over, to give a good shepherd wisdom in a desolate place, and then watching the desolate place — cleanup by cleanup, home by home, rain by rain — begin to come back to life.

The banyan put out its green in its own time, on no schedule anyone could rush. So has the island. The roots were down deeper than the fire could reach, and the water was there all along, and month after month, in an office most people will never see, we have been calling out for God to send it up. He has been sending it. Not as fast as our grief wanted, and not without cost, and mostly with no one watching. But He has been sending it. I have the list to prove it.

Chapter 10
The First Plant After the Fire

There is another plant I need to tell you about, and it is not the banyan.

I gave the banyan its own chapter because it is the tree everyone knows, the one that burned black and came back green, the survivor. But the banyan is a story about something that was already there and refused to die. A hundred years of roots were under it, reaching for water the fire could not touch. There is a different kind of miracle, though, and it is the one that comes to ground where nothing was — where there is no old root system waiting under the ash, no century of established life to draw on, only barren rock and the question of whether anything will ever grow there again.

On these islands we know that kind of ground. A lava flow leaves it behind. When the lava cools, what it leaves is not soil. It is black rock, hard and dry and empty, the most unpromising ground you can imagine, and you would think it would stay that way for a lifetime. But it does not. Something comes. And the first thing to come, the first green thing to take hold on bare lava where there is not yet any soil to speak of and hardly any water, is a plant called the aʻaliʻi.

The aʻaliʻi does not wait for the ground to be ready. That is the thing about it. It goes first. It takes root in rock, in the dry and the hard and the empty, and it does something that still sounds impossible to me when I say it out loud: it pulls water out of the air. When the ground has nothing to give, the aʻaliʻi draws its moisture from the atmosphere itself, out of the very dryness, and it lives. And because it lives, the ground behind it slowly becomes ground that other things can grow in. It is the pioneer. It is the first plant after the fire.

Someone decided to name a village after it.

It is called Aʻaliʻi Village, and it is a hundred and twenty-six homes rising on eight acres in Kahului. When I say rising, I mean it is being built, planned and drawn and worked toward, because a thing like this does not appear overnight; it is planted, and planted things take time, which is the lesson Jeremiah handed a scattered people from the start. But the shape of it is set, and the shape of it is what I want you to see, because it is the exchange I told you about in the tree, drawn up as a place where families can live.

Eighty-eight of those homes are affordable rentals. Now, you know what a rental usually is. It is a dead end with a door on it. You pay every month, and the money goes out into someone else's hands, and at the end of all those years of paying you own exactly nothing. You have kept a roof over your head and bought no piece of the ground under it. That is the arrangement most people on this island have made their peace with, because there was no other arrangement on offer, and after the fire, with a whole town's worth of families needing somewhere to live, even that arrangement got harder to reach.

Aʻaliʻi Village does something different with the rent. A portion of every payment does not vanish. It goes into a fund set aside in the tenant's name — an exit fund, they call it, though it is really an entrance. And the people living there are not only paying rent; they are learning. There is financial education built into the thing, the plain knowledge that most of us were never taught about how money is kept and grown and turned into ownership. And they are building with their own hands — sweat equity, work put into the place that becomes a stake in the place. So over time the money laid aside and the equity earned and the things learned all gather into a doorway, and the renter is meant to walk through that doorway as an owner. A person who lost a home is not simply handed another roof to rent until they die. They are handed a road back to owning one.

Sit with that against the ash for a moment. The fire took homes that families had held for generations, homes that were the whole of what some of them had to pass down. And the answer being planted is not a handout that sets a person back down exactly where the fire found them, dependent and renting and owning nothing. The answer is a trade that carries them further along than they were — renter into owner, tenant into landholder, a family with nothing turned, over years of faithful work, into a family with something to leave behind. That is beauty for ashes with a mortgage attached. It is the exchange written into a neighborhood.

And whoever named it understood what they were naming. Housing on hard ground, for people who lost everything, drawing life up where there seemed to be none left — the aʻaliʻi, the first plant after the flow, pulling water out of the dry air because the ground had none to give. I do not think you could find a better name if you searched for a year. The banyan came back because its roots were deep. The aʻaliʻi comes because it will grow where there are no roots at all, and make ground for everything that follows. Maui needed both pictures. The town that will rise again, and the new thing that will grow on the barren place where nothing stood before.

Not everything born out of that fire is the size of a village.

We built a studio. One small studio, for one person. There is a woman from Lahaina who lost her home in the fire, and who lost her parents, and we built her a small place to live and she is living in it. I will not dress that up, because it does not need dressing and it is not mine to embellish, and because I would rather tell it right than fill it in from memory.

*[FOR THE INTERVIEW: The studio — who is she, her connection to the church, how she lost her parents in the fire, and what it has meant to her to have a place of her own again?]*

I set the studio next to the village on purpose, because I do not want you to think God only works at the scale that makes a headline. A hundred and twenty-six homes is the planting of the Lord. So is one studio with one grieving woman in it who has a door to close at night. The exchange is the same size either way. It is measured by what a person brought, not by how many were served.

There is one more thing that was born, and it did not come out of the ground. It came out of a prayer I prayed with my own house still smelling of smoke.

Two weeks after the fire, in the middle of everything I have already told you about, the reading plan put me in Paul's second letter to Timothy, and I wrote down what I saw.

*From my journal, filed under August 23:*

"S- You therefore my son, be strong in the grace that is in Christ Jesus. And the things you have heard from me among many witnesses commit to faithful men who will be able to teach others also. Soldier, Athlete, Farmer. II Tim 2:1,2. O- Be a son. Be strong in Grace. Be teachable, Be transparent, Look for faithfulness, look for leadership. A- Sonship is the foundation. Mentorship happens in groups. Leadership happens in groups. P- God, please give me wisdom in this."

I have told you already that this same August prayer for wisdom later walked with me into the mayor's office, month after month. But that is not where it started. It started here, on the page, with Paul telling a young pastor to take what he had been given and hand it to faithful men who could teach others also — and with me, two weeks into the worst season of my life, asking God for the wisdom to do exactly that. I did not pray for the crowds to grow. I prayed to raise leaders. Faithful people I could pour into, who would pour into others, who would pour into others still. Paul lists three pictures in those verses — the soldier, the athlete, and the farmer — and I have thought since about how the farmer is the one who waits, who plants and tends and does not see the harvest for a long while, but who plants anyway because the harvest is coming.

I have watched that prayer answered in front of me. The leaders came. They are multiplying now, more of them than there were, taking on the work and raising up others behind them. We have Grace Groups going where there were none. And the plainest sign of it, the one you can count, is the baptisms. They have been doubling. Month after month there are people going down into the water and coming up new, more of them than the month before, and I did not manufacture that with a program. I asked God for wisdom to raise faithful people, two weeks into the ash, and the faithful people He raised are the ones now standing in the water with the new believers. The farmer waited. The harvest came.

I keep coming back to a phrase I wrote in the margin of a page early in that first year, when a group of us sat down to make sense of the aftermath. Pain produces. That was the whole of it, two words. Not pain destroys, though it does. Not pain ends things, though it ends a great many things. Pain produces. There is a reason Scripture reaches for the picture of childbirth when it wants to talk about the worst kind of anguish giving way to the best kind of joy — the pain that comes before a birth, natural and spiritual both. It is the most severe pain there is, and it is the pain that hands you a living thing on the other side of it. The travail produces. And I have watched an island in travail produce a village, a studio, a generation of leaders, a river of people going into the water — the planting of the Lord coming up green out of a place that three years ago was nothing but black.

Which is where Isaiah has been pointing the whole time, in the verse I wrote in my notebook in September with the town still smoking in my memory. Beauty for ashes, the oil of joy for mourning, the garment of praise for the spirit of heaviness — that they might be called trees of righteousness, the planting of the Lord, that He might be glorified. I used to read that as a promise to individual people, and it is. But I have come to see it is a promise to a whole place. Maui is becoming the thing Isaiah described. Not a town restored board for board to what it was; I have never told you that, and I am not going to start. Something else. A tree of righteousness planted where a fire came through. An island learning, house by house and leader by leader and baptism by baptism, to draw living water up out of ground everyone had written off.

The aʻaliʻi does not ask the lava for permission. It roots in the rock and it pulls water from the air and it goes first, so that everything else can follow. That is what I have watched being planted here. And I have come to believe it is what God is always doing with the ashes we bring Him — not sparing us the fire, and not handing back what the fire took, but planting, in the barren place, the first green thing, and then another, and then another, until the ground that could grow nothing is covered over with the life He traded us for our grief.

There is only one thing left for me to say about that trade, and about where you are meant to bring your own ashes. I will say it, and then I will let this book go quiet.

Epilogue
The Correct Exchange

I told you at the end of the last chapter that there was one thing left to say, and this is it. I have been circling it the whole book. I am going to set it down plainly now, and then I am going to let this go quiet.

Pain never leaves you the same.

I want to be careful with that sentence, because there is a way to hear it as a threat and it is not a threat. It is simply true. The fire came through my island and none of us who lived it are the people we were on the seventh of August. You do not get to keep the version of yourself who had not yet watched the video, had not yet sat on the sanctuary floor, had not yet stood at the funerals or caught himself joking at one. That person is gone the way the town is gone. Pain does not visit you and then withdraw and leave the furniture where it was. It changes you. It lowers the floor of you. After it, you feel everything from further down.

The holes were still in His hands.

When Jesus rose, He rose whole, and He could have risen without a mark on Him. He did not. The holes in His hands were there for Thomas to touch, and they will be there when we see Him. The wounds did not disappear in the resurrection. They were carried up into it. The glorified body is a scarred body, and the scars are not a failure of the healing; they are the proof of the love. That is the whole thing, right there, in the hands of the risen Christ. Suffering does not have to be erased to be redeemed. It gets carried through. It gets kept.

Pain changes us by increasing our capacity to love.

I watched it in two widows who housed a town. I watched it in a mother's love, the particular fierceness a woman has for the child she carried and bore in pain, a love that came up out of the very travail that produced it. And I watched it, finally, in the hands of Jesus, who loves us the way He does because of what those hands went through. Suffering increases our capacity to feel. That is the mechanism. That is the whole engine of it. You come out of the furnace able to feel more, not less.

At first, of course, what you feel is the pain. Only the pain. It fills the new depth completely and you cannot imagine anything else ever living down there. And here is where nearly everyone makes the turn that costs them everything. They try to get rid of it. They medicate it, hide it, hurry it, wall it off behind good reasons the way I did, do whatever it takes to stop feeling the thing that hurts. And it works, a little, for a while. The pain goes quieter. But something else goes quiet with it, in equal and opposite measure, because it was always one instrument and never two. The people who succeed in not feeling their grief have arranged, without meaning to, not to feel much of anything. They got rid of the ashes. And now, when God comes asking, they have nothing left in their hands to bring Him.

Because that is what He is asking for.

I know how strange it sounds. He is not asking you to throw the ashes away. He is asking you to bring them. He promises that if you give Him your ashes, He will give you beauty. If you give Him your mourning, the oil of joy. If you give Him your heaviness, the garment of praise. It is an exchange, and an exchange only works if you have something to hand over. The people who medicated it all away come to the counter empty. They spent the very thing they were supposed to trade.

But if you embrace the pain instead — if you let yourself feel it all the way down, and then you do not keep it, and you carry it to God with both hands open — then the same depth that pain carved in you becomes the depth from which joy comes up. In the exact measure that you can feel pain, you become able to feel joy, and love, and grace. That is why your pain has such value. It is not the obstacle to the good life on the far side of it. It is the raw material of it. It is what the beauty is made from.

So this is the whole of it, and it is the last thing I have to say to you.

You are holding ashes. I do not have to know your name or your fire to know that much, because everyone who has lived long enough is holding some. A town, or a marriage, or a child, or a body, or a plan for the rest of your life that did not come back from its last tour. Whatever it is, you have been carrying it, and the world has been telling you the whole time to put it down, to get past it, to heal from it and stop feeling it and finally be free.

Do not do that. The ashes have value. They are the most valuable thing you own, because they are the one thing God is asking you to bring Him.

Only bring them to the right place. That is the only instruction I have left. There are a thousand counters that will take your pain and give you back nothing, or worse than nothing. Bring it to the correct exchange. Bring it to God. Put the ashes in the hands that still have the holes in them, and let Him make the trade only He can make.

The roots were down deeper than the fire could reach. They always are. We just cannot see it until everything on the surface is gone.

Beauty for ashes. Bring Him yours.

Chapter 8
The Widows

There were two widows in our church, and for years I thought I knew what they were.

I thought they were the ones I was there to help. That is not a small thing, and I do not want to make it sound like one. A pastor spends a great deal of his life bending toward the people who have lost the most, and these two women had lost as much as anyone I knew. I carried them in my prayers the way you carry the wounded. When I thought of them, I felt compassion, and underneath the compassion I filed them, without ever meaning to, under a single word. Victims. It is the word that comes most naturally to a helper, because it tells him what his job is. Here is a person to whom a terrible thing happened. Stand near them. Be gentle. Do what you can.

I was wrong about the word. Not about the terrible things — those were real, and I will tell them — but about what the terrible things had made. It took a whole town burning down for me to see it, and when I saw it I had to go back and rewrite everything I thought I understood about the two of them, and about pain, and about what God does with the people it breaks.

The first woman lost her husband to the war.

He was a soldier, and it was his last tour in Afghanistan. That is the detail I keep turning over, because of what it does to the rest of the story. It was the last one. They were almost through it. They had made the plans that a couple makes when the hardest part is nearly behind them, the plans about the years they would finally get to spend together, the ordinary long life on the far side of the deployments, the three children they would raise in peace. She was pregnant with the last of those children when he went. And he never came back. The baby she was carrying would be born into a family that had already lost its father, and she would raise all three of them without the man she had made the plans with. There is a particular cruelty in a loss that comes at the very end, when the danger was supposed to be almost over. She lived that cruelty. She built a whole life on the other side of it.

The second loss I was there for myself, and I have never been able to set it down.

There was a man in one of my small groups. He loved to run. That was one of the things you knew about him, the way you know the plain good things about the people in your group — he ran. He had a wife and two young boys. And one night my phone rang at eleven-thirty, and the call was about him, and the strange, awful thing about it was the geography. He was at the bottom of the hill where I live. Right there. A drunk driver had hit him. I drove down my own hill in the dark toward a scene I could almost see from my house, and when I got there his wife was already there, and the police told me what they had to tell me, which was that he had been killed on impact. He had gone out to run, the way he had a thousand times, and he did not come home, and it happened at the foot of the road I drive every day.

Then came the part I would give almost anything not to have had to do. Somebody had to tell his boys. Somebody had to be the one to say to two young children that their father had died, that he was not coming back, that they would not see him again. I was that somebody. I have preached on death for most of my adult life, and I have stood at more gravesides than I can count, and I did not have a single word that was equal to the face of that little boy. He kept asking me why. Just that. Why. And I had no answer. There is no answer. I have never pretended to him or to anyone else that I had one. So I did the only thing that was left to do, which was to hold him and cry with him. That is all I had. A pastor with nothing to say, holding a fatherless boy at the bottom of a hill, weeping, because the question he was asking is the one question the whole world has never been able to answer.

Two women. One whose husband did not come home from a war that was nearly over. One whose husband did not come home from a run down the street. I felt for them both for years, and I would have told you, if you had asked, that these were two of the people in my church who most needed to be carried.

And then Lahaina burned, and I watched them pick up the whole town.

Both of them opened their homes. Not for a night, not as a gesture, but for many nights, to complete strangers — people off the burned coast whom they had never met, whom they owed nothing, who could not have paid them back if they had wanted to. These were not women with an abundance to spare. One of them, the one who lost her husband, took in more than forty people. Let me say that again, because when I first heard it I had to stop and count it out in my head. More than forty. In her home. And she had no husband to share the weight of it, and she had her own children to raise at the same time, and she did it anyway, night after night, a widow running a shelter with her own two hands while the rest of us with our two parents and our two incomes were still trying to figure out where to put people.

I asked them both, at different times, how they could do it. It was not a rhetorical question. I genuinely did not understand where a person finds that, after what they had been through, and I wanted to know. And they gave me, without having compared notes, the same answer. They said: we know what loss is. We have been there. We know what it is like. How can we not be there for these people who are going through the same thing.

How can we not be there.

I have thought about that sentence more than almost anything else anyone said to me in those years, because it turns the whole logic of suffering upside down. The world would tell you that a person who has lost that much has earned the right to keep whatever she has left. That she, of all people, should be excused, should be allowed to protect herself, should be the last one asked to give. She has suffered enough. Let someone with more to spare do the giving. That is how we reckon it. But these two women reckoned it the opposite way, and they were not being noble, and they were not straining after a virtue. They were simply telling me the truth about themselves. The loss had not emptied them. It had opened them. They looked at a family standing on the ash of their own home with the same question in their eyes that a fatherless boy had once asked me at the bottom of a hill, and where I had no answer, these women had a house. They had a couch. They had forty places to sleep. And they could not not give them, because they knew, from the inside, exactly what that family was feeling, and you cannot un-know a thing like that.

That is when I understood I had filed them under the wrong word all along.

They were not victims. Somewhere in the years between their worst nights and the fire, God had been at work in them, down where I could not see it, and He had grown something in the dark that I had completely missed. There was a strength in these two women that most people never come near, and it had not appeared in spite of what they suffered. It had been produced by it. The apostle says it plainly, that tribulation produces — it does not merely survive, it produces — and I had read that a hundred times and never watched it happen in front of me until I watched two widows shelter a town. The suffering had gone down into them like roots into dry ground, and it had reached a source, and the source had made them strong in a way that the rest of us, the unbroken ones, simply were not.

Which is the exchange I told you about in the last chapter, standing up and walking around in the shape of two women. Beauty for ashes. I had explained it as a theology, the small word for, the truth that our ashes have value to God and that He trades us for them. Here it was proven. These two had brought God the worst nights of their lives, the pregnant goodbye and the eleven-thirty phone call, and they had not medicated it away and they had not hidden it and they had not spent the intervening years merely coping. They had given it to Him. And He had given them back, in the very same measure, a capacity to love that most of us never develop, because most of us have never been broken deeply enough to grow it. Their pain had increased their capacity — that is the plainest way I know to say it. The depth the grief carved in them was the exact depth from which the love now poured. They could hold forty strangers because they had once been held by no one but God at the bottom of their own worst night, and they remembered what it was, and they could not stand to let anyone else be there alone.

It taught me something about people that I have not been able to unlearn. From the outside, everyone looks the same. You pass a woman in the grocery store and you have no idea. She looks like anyone. But we are not all the same. Some people are carrying, underneath the ordinary surface, a strength that was forged in a furnace you cannot see, and you would never know it until the day a furnace comes for everyone and you watch who runs toward the fire. When Lahaina burned, I found out who in my church had that hidden strength, and it was not always who I would have guessed. Over and over it was the ones I had been carrying. The ones I had filed under victim. They rose up while I was still reeling, and they carried more than I did, because they had already been where the whole island was now going, and they had come back from it changed.

And here is the part that undid me, quietly, in the middle of all of it. I realized that God was not only doing this in those two women. He was doing it in the whole church. He was doing it in me. The same strength that had been produced in a war widow and a runner's widow was being produced, right then, in a whole community of people who had lost a town — the beginning of something I would only see the full size of much later. The fire was not only destroying. Down where none of us could watch, it was growing something. It was making, out of an island of victims, an island of people who would one day be able to say to the next sufferer the thing my two widows said to me. We know what loss is. How can we not be there.

I had spent years thinking my job was to stand near the broken and be gentle. That is still part of it, and I will do it until I die. But the widows taught me that the broken are not only to be tended. Sometimes they are to be followed. Sometimes the person to learn from in a disaster is not the strong one who arrived unhurt to help. It is the one who was shattered years ago and has been quietly turning the shards into strength ever since, waiting, though none of us knew she was waiting, for the day the rest of us would finally need to know how it is done.

They knew how it was done. They had been to the bottom of the hill. And when the whole island ended up down there in the dark, asking the same unanswerable why, these two women were already there, with the lights on and the doors open, holding whoever came, because someone had once held them, and they had never forgotten what it was worth.

Chapter 10
The First Plant After the Fire

There is another plant I need to tell you about, and it is not the banyan.

I gave the banyan its own chapter because it is the tree everyone knows, the one that burned black and came back green, the survivor. But the banyan is a story about something that was already there and refused to die. A hundred years of roots were under it, reaching for water the fire could not touch. There is a different kind of miracle, though, and it is the one that comes to ground where nothing was — where there is no old root system waiting under the ash, no century of established life to draw on, only barren rock and the question of whether anything will ever grow there again.

On these islands we know that kind of ground. A lava flow leaves it behind. When the lava cools, what it leaves is not soil. It is black rock, hard and dry and empty, the most unpromising ground you can imagine, and you would think it would stay that way for a lifetime. But it does not. Something comes. And the first thing to come, the first green thing to take hold on bare lava where there is not yet any soil to speak of and hardly any water, is a plant called the aʻaliʻi.

The aʻaliʻi does not wait for the ground to be ready. That is the thing about it. It goes first. It takes root in rock, in the dry and the hard and the empty, and it does something that still sounds impossible to me when I say it out loud: it pulls water out of the air. When the ground has nothing to give, the aʻaliʻi draws its moisture from the atmosphere itself, out of the very dryness, and it lives. And because it lives, the ground behind it slowly becomes ground that other things can grow in. It is the pioneer. It is the first plant after the fire.

Someone decided to name a village after it.

It is called Aʻaliʻi Village, and it is a hundred and twenty-six homes rising on eight acres in Kahului. When I say rising, I mean it is being built, planned and drawn and worked toward, because a thing like this does not appear overnight; it is planted, and planted things take time, which is the lesson Jeremiah handed a scattered people from the start. But the shape of it is set, and the shape of it is what I want you to see, because it is the exchange I told you about in the tree, drawn up as a place where families can live.

Eighty-eight of those homes are affordable rentals. Now, you know what a rental usually is. It is a dead end with a door on it. You pay every month, and the money goes out into someone else's hands, and at the end of all those years of paying you own exactly nothing. You have kept a roof over your head and bought no piece of the ground under it. That is the arrangement most people on this island have made their peace with, because there was no other arrangement on offer, and after the fire, with a whole town's worth of families needing somewhere to live, even that arrangement got harder to reach.

Aʻaliʻi Village does something different with the rent. A portion of every payment does not vanish. It goes into a fund set aside in the tenant's name — an exit fund, they call it, though it is really an entrance. And the people living there are not only paying rent; they are learning. There is financial education built into the thing, the plain knowledge that most of us were never taught about how money is kept and grown and turned into ownership. And they are building with their own hands — sweat equity, work put into the place that becomes a stake in the place. So over time the money laid aside and the equity earned and the things learned all gather into a doorway, and the renter is meant to walk through that doorway as an owner. A person who lost a home is not simply handed another roof to rent until they die. They are handed a road back to owning one.

Sit with that against the ash for a moment. The fire took homes that families had held for generations, homes that were the whole of what some of them had to pass down. And the answer being planted is not a handout that sets a person back down exactly where the fire found them, dependent and renting and owning nothing. The answer is a trade that carries them further along than they were — renter into owner, tenant into landholder, a family with nothing turned, over years of faithful work, into a family with something to leave behind. That is beauty for ashes with a mortgage attached. It is the exchange written into a neighborhood.

And whoever named it understood what they were naming. Housing on hard ground, for people who lost everything, drawing life up where there seemed to be none left — the aʻaliʻi, the first plant after the flow, pulling water out of the dry air because the ground had none to give. I do not think you could find a better name if you searched for a year. The banyan came back because its roots were deep. The aʻaliʻi comes because it will grow where there are no roots at all, and make ground for everything that follows. Maui needed both pictures. The town that will rise again, and the new thing that will grow on the barren place where nothing stood before.

Not everything born out of that fire is the size of a village.

We built a studio. One small studio, for one person. There is a woman from Lahaina who lost her home in the fire, and who lost her parents, and we built her a small place to live and she is living in it. I will not dress that up, because it does not need dressing and it is not mine to embellish, and because I would rather tell it right than fill it in from memory.

I set the studio next to the village on purpose, because I do not want you to think God only works at the scale that makes a headline. A hundred and twenty-six homes is the planting of the Lord. So is one studio with one grieving woman in it who has a door to close at night. The exchange is the same size either way. It is measured by what a person brought, not by how many were served.

There is one more thing that was born, and it did not come out of the ground. It came out of a prayer I prayed with my own house still smelling of smoke.

Two weeks after the fire, in the middle of everything I have already told you about, the reading plan put me in Paul's second letter to Timothy, and I wrote down what I saw.

*From my journal, filed under August 23:*

"S- You therefore my son, be strong in the grace that is in Christ Jesus. And the things you have heard from me among many witnesses commit to faithful men who will be able to teach others also. Soldier, Athlete, Farmer. II Tim 2:1,2. O- Be a son. Be strong in Grace. Be teachable, Be transparent, Look for faithfulness, look for leadership. A- Sonship is the foundation. Mentorship happens in groups. Leadership happens in groups. P- God, please give me wisdom in this."

I have told you already that this same August prayer for wisdom later walked with me into the mayor's office, month after month. But that is not where it started. It started here, on the page, with Paul telling a young pastor to take what he had been given and hand it to faithful men who could teach others also — and with me, two weeks into the worst season of my life, asking God for the wisdom to do exactly that. I did not pray for the crowds to grow. I prayed to raise leaders. Faithful people I could pour into, who would pour into others, who would pour into others still. Paul lists three pictures in those verses — the soldier, the athlete, and the farmer — and I have thought since about how the farmer is the one who waits, who plants and tends and does not see the harvest for a long while, but who plants anyway because the harvest is coming.

I have watched that prayer answered in front of me. The leaders came. They are multiplying now, more of them than there were, taking on the work and raising up others behind them. We have Grace Groups going where there were none. And the plainest sign of it, the one you can count, is the baptisms. They have been doubling. Month after month there are people going down into the water and coming up new, more of them than the month before, and I did not manufacture that with a program. I asked God for wisdom to raise faithful people, two weeks into the ash, and the faithful people He raised are the ones now standing in the water with the new believers. The farmer waited. The harvest came.

I keep coming back to a phrase I wrote in the margin of a page early in that first year, when a group of us sat down to make sense of the aftermath. Pain produces. That was the whole of it, two words. Not pain destroys, though it does. Not pain ends things, though it ends a great many things. Pain produces. There is a reason Scripture reaches for the picture of childbirth when it wants to talk about the worst kind of anguish giving way to the best kind of joy — the pain that comes before a birth, natural and spiritual both. It is the most severe pain there is, and it is the pain that hands you a living thing on the other side of it. The travail produces. And I have watched an island in travail produce a village, a studio, a generation of leaders, a river of people going into the water — the planting of the Lord coming up green out of a place that three years ago was nothing but black.

Which is where Isaiah has been pointing the whole time, in the verse I wrote in my notebook in September with the town still smoking in my memory. Beauty for ashes, the oil of joy for mourning, the garment of praise for the spirit of heaviness — that they might be called trees of righteousness, the planting of the Lord, that He might be glorified. I used to read that as a promise to individual people, and it is. But I have come to see it is a promise to a whole place. Maui is becoming the thing Isaiah described. Not a town restored board for board to what it was; I have never told you that, and I am not going to start. Something else. A tree of righteousness planted where a fire came through. An island learning, house by house and leader by leader and baptism by baptism, to draw living water up out of ground everyone had written off.

The aʻaliʻi does not ask the lava for permission. It roots in the rock and it pulls water from the air and it goes first, so that everything else can follow. That is what I have watched being planted here. And I have come to believe it is what God is always doing with the ashes we bring Him — not sparing us the fire, and not handing back what the fire took, but planting, in the barren place, the first green thing, and then another, and then another, until the ground that could grow nothing is covered over with the life He traded us for our grief.

There is only one thing left for me to say about that trade, and about where you are meant to bring your own ashes. I will say it, and then I will let this book go quiet.

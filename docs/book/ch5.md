Chapter 5
The Night the Oil Ran Dry

Jesus told a story once about ten young women waiting for a bridegroom.

They all had lamps and they were all waiting for the same thing. Five were wise and five were foolish, and the only difference between the two groups — the whole difference, the entire point of the parable — was oil. The wise ones brought extra. The foolish ones brought only what was already burning in the lamp. Then the bridegroom was delayed, longer than any of them had planned for, and while they waited the lamps burned low, and at midnight, when the cry finally went up that he was coming, the foolish women looked down and saw their flames going out and had nothing left to pour in.

I have read that parable my whole life and always thought it was about being ready. It is. But there is something underneath the readiness that I did not see until Lahaina, and it is this: the danger is not the fire. The danger is the length of the wait. The foolish women did not fail because a crisis came. They failed because the crisis lasted longer than their supply. They ran out in the middle of the night, and there is no store open in the middle of the night, and you cannot borrow oil from someone else, because in a season like that everyone is running their own lamp down to the wick.

Nobody warns you about the oil when the fire starts.

At the start there is so much of it you cannot imagine ever running low. In those first days I told you about, the truckloads and the checks and the sanctuary floor full of sleeping families, I was not tired. I want to be honest about that, because it is the part that fools you. Crisis comes with its own strange fuel. The adrenaline is real, and the purpose is real, and you get up every morning knowing exactly what to do and why it matters, and there is a kind of terrible clarity in it that feels almost like strength. You are not counting the oil because the lamp is burning bright and the flame is high and the work is holy and you cannot conceive of the night lasting long enough to matter.

But the night lasts.

There is a language for what happens to a community after a disaster, and I learned it the way you learn any language, by living inside it. Rescue. Relief. Recovery. Those are the stages, and they do not go by in a week. Rescue is the frantic part, the part the cameras come for, the part where you are pulling people toward safety and getting water and gas and medicine to the ones running toward the flames. Then relief, the long feeding and clothing and sheltering of people who have nothing, which does not end when the news trucks leave. Then recovery, which is the longest of all, the slow grinding forward through insurance and housing and grief and the ten thousand small ruined things that have to be rebuilt one at a time. You do not move through those stages in order and then rest. You live in all of them at once, for six months, for twelve, on high tilt the entire time, and the tilt becomes so normal that you forget a person is not built to stand at that angle.

And through the whole of it, there were the funerals.

There were a great many of them, in those months. People lost family in that fire, and the living had to bury them, and a pastor is one of the people who stands up front when that happens. I did that work over and over across that year, and I would not trade having done it, because it is one of the truest things a pastor is ever given to do, to stand with a family in the worst hour of their lives and speak the name of the God who holds the dead. But there is a cost to it that accrues quietly, one service at a time, and I was not counting the cost any more than I was counting the oil.

I did not see what it was doing to me until a single moment at a single funeral.

I was standing there talking with someone who had just lost their father. And I noticed, as if from somewhere outside myself, the tone that was coming out of my mouth. I was almost joking with them. Light. Easy. The way you talk to somebody at a party, at a barbecue, at some happy gathering where nothing is at stake. This was a grave, and a grieving child, and I was chatting like it was a good afternoon. And even as it was happening I felt a cold drop of recognition go through me, because that is not who I am and that is not how I hold the grieving. Something had gone numb. Something had worn through. I stood at that funeral making light conversation over a fresh loss, and I understood, quietly and completely, that something in me was broken.

I did not have a name for it that day. I found the name later, and the name is compassion fatigue. It is what happens to helpers, to nurses and chaplains and first responders and pastors, when they pour out compassion faster than it can be replenished, day after day, with no time to refill. The well does not announce that it is going dry. You keep lowering the bucket and one day it comes up with less, and then less than that, and then one afternoon you catch yourself laughing at a graveside and realize the bucket is scraping the bottom. The oil in the lamp is nearly gone, and you are still standing there in the dark expecting it to burn.

There was something else riding underneath it that I could not name for a long while either, and it was a strange kind of guilt. My house did not burn. I told you already, in the chapter before this one, that I gave myself no permission to grieve because everyone in front of me had lost more than I had, and that was true, and it was its own trap. But there was a second thing braided into it. There is a particular ache that belongs to the ones who came through, the ones who still have their homes and their families intact, standing in front of people who lost everything. Survival guilt, they call it. You feel that your wholeness is somehow an offense in the presence of their ruin. So you work harder, to earn the standing you have no way to earn, and you drive the lamp lower, and you tell yourself that the exhaustion is the least you owe.

That is the condition I carried into a retreat, many months after the fire.

It was a gathering of pastors — men who had been doing what I had been doing, standing where I had been standing, all across that same wounded island. Somebody had the wisdom to build it around rest. Rest, review, recalibrate, reconnect. To reconnect with God, and with our wives, and with each other. And to sit down in a room together and finally say out loud, to people who would understand it, what the year had actually cost us.

I remember a piece of it that has stayed with me. Someone asked what advice we would give to a pastor six months out from a disaster, and the answer that came back was not soft. Buckle up. It is not over. Build deep with God and with others. That was it. Not a promise that the hard part had passed, but a warning that it had not, and a single instruction for surviving what remained: go deep, with God and with the people around you, because shallow will not hold under this weight. We talked about staff spread thin. About managing what little energy we had left. About marriages and friendships strained by the tilt. About survival guilt and compassion fatigue, the two things I had been carrying without a name, spoken plainly by other men who were carrying them too.

And in that room, among those pastors, I broke down.

I had spent the whole year as the one who held other people while they wept. I had stood at the funerals and sat on the sanctuary floor and prayed with the ones whose losses were larger than mine, and I had never once let it be about me, because I did not believe it was allowed to be. But in that room it finally was. These were not people I was serving. They were people who knew, from the inside, exactly what I had been through, and there was nothing left in front of me to protect them from. So I said it — that I was struggling, that something in me was broken, that I needed help myself — and it came apart, and I wept, and the room held me the way I had spent a year holding everyone else. It was healing, and not only for me. It was healing for all of us, because every man in that room had been running his lamp down to the wick and telling himself he was fine.

That was the night I understood the parable differently.

The ten virgins were not condemned for waiting. They were all waiting; that was the whole assignment. They were divided by oil. And I had been living like one of the foolish ones, burning what was in the lamp and bringing nothing extra, because the crisis felt too urgent to stop and refill. But the extra oil is not a luxury. The extra oil is the point. You have to be prayed up, filled up, rested up, before the long night, because you cannot manufacture any of those things at midnight when the flame is already guttering. I had learned it the hard way. I had run out of oil at a graveside and heard it in my own voice.

There was a line I wrote down in those days that I have carried ever since. Three short pieces of it. Life is beautiful. Lahaina is gone. Everything is temporary. I set them side by side because they are all true at the same time, and you have to hold all three or you will go mad holding only one. Life is beautiful — you cannot let the fire talk you out of that, and there is beauty even in the ash, even at the graveside, even in a room full of broken pastors weeping. Lahaina is gone — you cannot pretend otherwise, cannot spiritualize the loss into something smaller than it was, cannot skip the grief to get to the comfort. And everything is temporary — the town, the pain, the tilt, the very oil in the lamp, all of it passing through your hands and none of it staying. Don't blink, I wrote, because a life is short and a beautiful thing can be gone in a night, and I had watched a hundred-year-old town go in a night, and I did not want to sleepwalk through whatever was left.

I came off that retreat knowing I needed rest, and knowing something more than that. I was beginning to understand, though I could not yet have explained it to you, that the pain I had been trying to outwork was not something to be cured or solved or hurried past. There was a truth waiting on the far side of that exhaustion about what God actually does with our brokenness, and I was only at the edge of it. That truth belongs to a later chapter, and I will get there.

But I want to end this one where the parable ends, in the dark, at the sound of the cry. The foolish women went off to buy oil and missed the very thing they had waited all night for. The wise ones had brought enough to last the length of the wait. I do not tell you this as a man who was wise. I tell it to you as one of the foolish ones who ran dry at midnight and was carried, by the mercy of God and the arms of other broken men, back to a place where the lamp could be filled again. The oil ran out. That is the honest name of that season. But the night was not the end of me, and the running dry turned out to be the beginning of learning where the oil comes from in the first place.

I had been carrying far too much of it alone. I was about to find out, in a room with my wife, just how much.

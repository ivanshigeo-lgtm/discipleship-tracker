Chapter 2
Everyone's Town

There is a thing people who don't live on islands have a hard time understanding.

On the mainland a town can burn and the next town over will read about it in the news. Here it does not work that way. Maui is one island, and one island is one neighborhood, however many miles of road lie between its ends. The man who fixes your car went to school with the woman who ran the plate lunch place in the town that burned. Your kids play ball against their kids. The person who lost everything is not a stranger you watched on a screen. He is somebody's cousin, and on Maui everybody is somebody's cousin eventually. There is no such thing here as someone else's disaster.

That is what I mean when I say Lahaina was everyone's town. I told you already what it was to me, the weekends and the surf and the memories stitched in over a lifetime, and I don't need to say all that again. But Lahaina was not only mine. It belonged to the whole island the way a heart belongs to a whole body. It was the old capital, the historic front of the west side, the harbor and the streets that every family on Maui had walked through at one time or another. Weddings happened there. Anniversaries. The ordinary Saturdays that a life is actually made of. When people talk about Lahaina they are not really talking about buildings. They are talking about the place where a good part of the island kept its memories. So when it went, it did not go for a few thousand residents. It went for all of us. Every person on Maui lost something in that fire, even the ones whose houses never caught.

I have said the loss does not fit inside your chest, and it doesn't. You could not take it in as a total. You could only take it in one family at a time. A father whose boat was sunk and burned, a mother whose school was gone, a home destroyed, children with no school left standing — four whole tragedies in a single evening, in a single household, and that was one family among thousands. Multiply that grief across a town and your mind refuses the arithmetic. It will not hold. So you stop trying to hold the whole thing, and you do the only thing a person can do, which is to reach the family in front of you.

For us that meant the church.

I told you we opened Grace Bible Church for donations, but I want you to see what that actually became, because it was more than a drop-off point. It became a store where nothing had a price. People brought what they had, and people took what they needed, and no money changed hands and no one asked a single question at the door. You walked in, and if you needed diapers you took diapers, and if you needed clothes you took clothes, and if you needed food you took food, and you walked out again carried by the generosity of strangers who would never know your name. That is a strange and holy thing to watch, a room full of goods flowing in one door and out the other with no ledger in between. It runs against everything the world trains us to do with our possessions. And for a while, in that building, it was simply how things worked.

But a free store closes at night, and the need did not.

So we let people sleep in the church. Whole families came in off a burned coast with nowhere in the world to lay their heads, and we gave them the sanctuary floor and were glad to. And here I have to tell you something that still sits oddly with me when I say it out loud. We slept there too. My own family slept in that building, night after night, for security. The church was full of donated goods and full of people who had lost everything, and someone needed to be there through the dark hours, so we were there. There is a picture in my mind I don't think will ever leave, of the room where I preach on Sundays turned into a shelter, bodies on the floor in the quiet, and my own kids among them. I had spent years standing at the front of that room talking about the love of God. For those weeks the room itself was the sermon, and I was only one of the people asleep in it.

It was not only the church. The homes of our people opened too. Families in the congregation who had their houses still standing threw their doors wide and took in people they had never met, put them up in spare rooms and on couches and in whatever space they had, for many nights in those early days when there was simply nowhere else for anyone to go. Nobody organized it from a stage. It just happened, the way water finds the low ground. Somebody had a need and somebody else had a room, and the two found each other, and that was that. I watched my own congregation become housing before there was any housing to be had. Some of them are people I will tell you about later, and when you meet them you will understand that the ones who opened their homes widest were often the ones who had themselves been through the deepest loss. But that is a chapter for further on.

For three, four months, that was our life. Sitting with people who had lost everything. Praying with them. Being close enough to hear what was needed and then trying to meet it. There was no plan for a season like that, no manual, and honestly no rest. You did not go home at the end of the day and set it down, because for a long stretch there was no clear line between the day and the night, or between the church and the home, or between the helpers and the helped. We were all just inside the same fire together, doing the next thing that needed doing.

And some of the next things were harder to reach than others.

There is one I have to tell you about now, though I have to tell it plainly, because I was in the middle of it and I still could not give you every piece of it if you asked. Lahaina is on the far side of the West Maui mountains, connected to the rest of us by a road that hugs the coast, and in those first days that road did not always cooperate. There were times we simply could not get supplies through by land. Our caravan of trucks, loaded and ready, could not make the crossing. And so we did the thing the situation asked of us, which was to stop trying to force the road and put the supplies on the water instead. When we couldn't get to Lahaina by caravan, we got there by boat.

I wish I could set the whole scene for you the way I have set the others — what we carried, whose boat it was, where we landed on that ruined shore, whose hands received it on the other end. I was part of it and it happened and there are photographs of it, and I would rather leave the details to be told right than dress them up here from memory.

What I can tell you is what it meant. It meant the help got there anyway. When the road said no, the water said yes, and the supplies that could not go by wheel went by hull, and reached people who could not have known or cared by what route their food and water arrived, only that it came. There is a lesson buried in that, and it is the same lesson as the influencers and the checks I told you about before. The help finds a way. You do not always get to choose the road. You only have to refuse to quit when one road closes, and trust that another one is opening while you are still standing at the barricade.

I keep coming back to those two words. Everyone's town. Because it worked in both directions, and that is the part I did not fully see until later. Lahaina belonged to all of us, which is why all of us grieved it. But the response belonged to all of us too. It was not the church that fed the west side, and it was not the influencers, and it was not any one hero you could name and thank. It was a whole island turning toward one wounded corner of itself. Boats and trucks and spare rooms and sanctuary floors and five-hundred-dollar checks passed hand to hand by people who knew the people. When a heart is hurt, the whole body sends what it has to the wound. That is not charity. That is just what a body does, if it is alive.

I did not know yet, in those crowded months, what all of it was for. I did not know that some of the people sleeping on that floor and opening those homes were being prepared for something, or that the town in ashes was the beginning of an exchange I could not yet read. I only knew that everyone's town had burned, and that everyone had come.

That turned out to be the first shoot of green, though none of us called it that at the time. We were too tired to name it. We just kept showing up, one family at a time, on whatever road was open that day.

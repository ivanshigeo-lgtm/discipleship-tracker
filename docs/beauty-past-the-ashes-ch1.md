Beauty Past the Ashes: Lahaina
*The Exchange God Makes with Our Grief*

Chapter One
The Speed of Trust

There was a red glow across the clouds that evening.

You live on Maui long enough and you learn the moods of the sky, and this one was wrong. The smell came with it, smoke drifting across the West Maui mountains and settling over the whole island like a lid on a pot. People were saying things. That there were wildfires in Lahaina. That major structures were burning. Nobody could tell you how much or how bad, only that it was happening, and that the wind would not quit.

It was not only the west side. Up in Kula the fire was doing something it had never done in the history of that place, tearing through the upcountry like it had a mind of its own. Kihei was evacuated. The fire came down off the mountain and kept coming until it stopped about twenty yards from the edge of the Kihei district, and a whole town stood still and watched it decide. You could feel the island holding its breath. And then the night closed over everything, and we went to sleep not knowing, because there was nothing else to do but wait for the morning.

The morning came, and with it a video.

A friend of mine flies helicopters. He sent me footage he had taken from the air. I watched it, and then I watched it again, because the first time my mind would not accept what my eyes were telling me. Lahaina was gone. Not damaged. Gone. Smoke rising off the ashes of a town that had stood for more than a hundred years, the historic heart of the west side, the place that gave that whole coast its allure and drew something like a quarter of the tourists who come to Maui. Block after block of it, black. I could not believe what I was seeing.

I want to tell you what Lahaina was before I tell you what we did, because you cannot understand the second thing without the first.

Maui is a small place. I did not live in Lahaina, but distance does not mean much on an island this size. Everyone's town is your town. I spent weekends over there my whole life. Dates with my wife, walking Front Street, the two of us with the water on one side. My kids in the sand at the beach. My high school years out in the surf with my friends, paddling out at spots I could still find with my eyes closed. Lahaina was stitched into my memory the way a place is when you have loved it in every season of your life. It was a place I loved. When I watched that video, I was not watching a news story about strangers. I was watching part of my own life burn.

And I knew, the way you sometimes know a thing all at once and without argument, that we had to do something.

So we opened the church. Grace Bible Church became a place where people could bring what they had and take what they needed, no cost, no questions. Donations came in, and we started helping in the only way that made sense in those first hours, which was to feed the people who were running toward the fire when everyone else was running away. We got meals to our first responders. And as we put our hands to work, we started to hear. That is how it goes. You do not learn what people need by planning it in a meeting. You learn it by showing up, by being close enough that they can tell you. Water. Medical supplies. Food. Gas. So we told our community what was needed, and our community answered, and we began collecting truckloads of supplies and moving them where they had to go.

Then came the stories.

I heard about one family. The father worked on a boat, and the boat was sunk and burned. The mother worked at a school, and the school burned to the ground. Their home was destroyed. And the children's school no longer existed. Any one of those things would be enough to break a family for a year. This family lived through four of them in a single evening. And they were not alone. All over the west side, people had lost everything they owned, and many of them had lost people too, family members who did not make it out. The scale of it does not fit inside your chest. You cannot hold it. You can only start somewhere.

Where we started was our own people.

We decided to get money into hands as fast as we could, and we decided we were not going to wait behind a wall of paperwork to do it. Most organizations, and for good reason, build a process to protect themselves from fraud, and that process takes time, and time was the one thing the people of Lahaina did not have. We had something else. We had the speed of relationships. We had the speed of trust. Our congregation was full of people who personally knew families who had lost everything, so we put the checks in the hands of the people who knew the people. Five hundred dollars at a time, through members who could look someone in the eye and say, this is for you. In short order we had written out more than a hundred thousand dollars that way.

I will be honest, we did not know how it would land. Five hundred dollars against a burned-down life is nothing. It felt almost like an insult to the size of the loss. I worried about how people would take it. But our members came back with the same report, over and over. People received those checks with gratitude. With tears. Not because the amount solved anything, but because someone had seen them, and reached them, and reached them fast.

Around that time I started hearing about the influencers. People online raising money for Maui, hundreds of thousands of dollars flowing to accounts with big followings, and folks kept telling me we needed to do the same, we needed to get out there and post and campaign and pull in those kinds of numbers. I prayed about it. And what I heard back was clear enough that I have never forgotten it. God told me those influencers were raising money for us. That if we did not get distracted, if we just kept our heads down and kept helping the community in front of us, the money would find its way to us in the end.

So that is what we did. We did not have enough. We kept giving anyway. And as we gave, more came in. It kept pouring in. We ended up putting five hundred dollars each into the hands of more than a thousand families, and we cleared half a million dollars in the first couple of months alone. Then the need changed, the way a wound changes as it moves through its stages. First it was water and food and gas. Then it was housing. And the funds shifted with it, and we were able to help churches set up feeding kitchens, help rebuild what had burned, help people get back on their feet. But that is further down the road than this chapter goes. For now I only want you to see the shape of those first weeks: a town in ashes, and a people moving at the speed of trust, and money arriving from strangers exactly the way I had been told it would.

Which brings me to my journal.

I keep my devotions in a simple form. Scripture, observation, application, prayer. I have done it for years. And in an ordinary season I write it most mornings and the dates line up neatly, one day after another. But that fall was not an ordinary season, and I fell behind. For months I fell behind. There was no room in those days for a quiet notebook. There were people sleeping in the church and people I was sitting with in their sorrow and truckloads to move and checks to write, and the mornings got swallowed one after another.

I have a rule I keep even when everything else is coming apart. I do not skip. I catch up. When I finally get an hour, I sit down and I write the days I missed, and I date each entry to the day it belongs to, so the sequence stays true even when the calendar and the ink no longer agree. The dates lag, but the order never breaks.

So there are pages in my journal marked early July of 2023 that were not written in July at all. They were written in September, from inside the aftermath, with Lahaina still smoking in my memory. I was walking through Jeremiah in those weeks, and I have thought about that many times since, because I did not choose the book to fit the moment. The reading plan chose it, months before there was anything to grieve. And there was the prophet, writing to a scattered people from inside a catastrophe, telling them to build houses and plant gardens in the land of their exile, because the rebuild was going to take time.

*From my journal, filed under July 8:*

"All pain and suffering is temporary. God, thank you for giving to me a perspective that is longer than the next few years. Help me to see the good that you have prepared for the many inhabitants of Lahaina and for Maui. Help me to walk with faith and not with fear knowing you are with me."

And then the next page, the one I have come to think of as the seed of everything that followed. Jeremiah is in prison, and God tells him to call out and be shown great and mighty things he does not yet know. I sat with that verse in September, thinking about my own loss and the loss of a whole town, and I wrote a prayer without any idea that I was naming a book.

*From my journal, filed under July 9:*

"In my loss and the loss of Lahaina, God wants me to call on him to show the great and mighty things. God help me to see the beauty past the ashes, that we might be trees of righteousness that He should be glorified."

Beauty past the ashes. I did not invent the phrase and I did not know at the time that I would build a book around it. It came out of Isaiah, and it came out of the ground of Lahaina, and it came out of the one thing in that burned town that would not let go of hope.

There was a banyan tree.

It stood down by the harbor and had stood there for more than a hundred years, one of the great landmarks of the place, the tree everyone photographed, the tree under which a whole town's worth of ordinary afternoons had happened. When the fire came through, it burned like everything else. It went black. And in the weeks after, people would look at it and wonder out loud whether it was dead, whether anything that scorched could ever come back, whether there was any hope left in it at all.

Then, months later, the first green shoots appeared.

The roots had gone down deep enough, over all those years, to find water the tree could not see. Everything above ground was ash and everything around it was dry, and still, down in the dark, it had been holding onto a source. And when the green came, the whole town understood what it meant without anyone having to explain it. If that tree could rise out of the ashes, maybe Lahaina could too.

That is where this book is going. I will get to the banyan tree properly later, and I will get to the widows in my church who I once thought of as victims and learned to see as heroes, and to the funerals, and to the day I found something in me had broken and needed mending. Those are stories for the chapters ahead.

But I want to end this first chapter where the prophet ended and where Isaiah pointed, because it is the truth underneath all the rest. Isaiah wrote that God would give beauty for ashes, the oil of joy for mourning, the garment of praise for the spirit of heaviness, that we would be called trees of righteousness, the planting of the Lord, that He might be glorified. The whole thing turns on one small word. For. He gives us something for something. It is an exchange.

Which means the ashes are not worthless. They have value to Him. He is asking for them.

I did not understand that yet, standing there in September with a helicopter video still burning behind my eyes and a town still smoking and a notebook full of days I was only now catching up on. But I had already written the prayer. Help me to see the beauty past the ashes. And He was already answering it, one green shoot at a time.
